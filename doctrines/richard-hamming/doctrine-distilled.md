# The Hamming Doctrine

> "What are the important problems of your field? Why aren't you working on them?"

**About:** A three-layer filter for choosing and executing research and technical work. Distilled from 'You and Your Research' (Bell Labs lecture, 1986), 'The Art of Doing Science and Engineering: Learning to Learn' (NPS lectures, 1995-1998), Hamming's Turing Award lecture (1968), Bell Labs operating history (1945-1986), and the error-correcting codes paper (1950) as a case study in the doctrine itself.

---
## Layer 1 — Problem-Selection Taste
*What is worth working on at all*

Hamming's central observation: most scientists and engineers spend their careers on safe, publishable, forgettable problems. The discipline of problem selection — asking not 'what can I solve?' but 'what would matter?' — separates great scientists from good ones. This layer encodes Hamming's criteria for identifying problems worth a career.

### The Monday Morning Question
**Rule:** Every Monday morning ask: 'What are the important problems of my field?' Then ask: 'Why am I not working on them?' Most people have never seriously posed either.

**Test:** Can the researcher name the top three important problems of their field unprompted, AND give a concrete reason (capability gap, time, courage) for not currently working on each? Vague answers fail.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Importance Before Tractability
**Rule:** Start with importance — what would matter? — then find tractability. Working on an unimportant problem perfectly is wasted work.

**Test:** Ask the researcher to state why their current problem is important before they explain how they're solving it. If importance is an afterthought or qualified with "it's a stepping stone," the selection filter failed.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science, Ch. 1

### The Great Scientist vs. Good Scientist Test
**Rule:** The difference is not intelligence but courage: to work on important problems, to change fields when necessary, to think long-term about what matters.

**Test:** Has the researcher ever abandoned a safe productive line of work to attack a harder, more important problem where they risked failure? Absence of this pattern is a signal of career-safety optimization.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Courage Is the Prerequisite
**Rule:** It takes courage to work on important problems because they have a real chance of failure. Courage, not genius, separates great scientists.

**Test:** Is the researcher currently working on something where failure is a real possibility and where that failure would be visible to peers? If every current project is likely to produce a publishable result, courage is absent.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Reasonable Chance Criterion
**Rule:** Important problems must have a non-trivial probability of being solvable with available tools. Both importance AND tractability are required.

**Test:** Can the researcher articulate a plausible path — not guaranteed, but credible — from current capabilities to a solution within a 3-5 year horizon? No credible path = not yet tractable.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Open Door vs. Closed Door Problems
**Rule:** Slightly open doors are the richest territory. The crack in the door is the secret: a technique, dataset, or framing that makes it tractable for you but not for others.

**Test:** What specific resource, technique, or insight does this researcher have that makes a hard problem slightly open for them specifically? If the answer applies equally to any researcher in the field, there is no crack.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Plant Seeds Broadly, Tend the Ones That Grow
**Rule:** Maintain a portfolio of 5-10 problems in the back of your mind. When one becomes tractable, you'll be positioned to move fast.

**Test:** Ask the researcher to name 5 problems they're monitoring but not actively attacking. If they cannot, the seed portfolio is empty and they will miss windows of tractability.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science

### The 10% Rule
**Rule:** Invest roughly 10% of working time in long-shot, potentially transformative problems even with no clear path forward.

**Test:** In the last month, how many hours were spent on exploratory problems with no clear deliverable? Zero hours = the long-shot portfolio is unfunded.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Reject Safe Mediocrity
**Rule:** Choosing problems to guarantee a result optimizes for career safety at the cost of career significance. The choice to be safe is a choice to be forgettable.

**Test:** If the researcher's current work succeeds, would a reasonable colleague describe it as "a solid contribution" rather than "important"? Solid contributions are the safe choice.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Think About the Next Problem During This One
**Rule:** Great scientists discover the next problem while working on the current one. Maintain a parallel thread: what does this result generalize to?

**Test:** Ask the researcher: "If this project succeeds in the next 6 months, what does it enable or point toward?" If they have no answer, the generalization thread is absent.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Long-View Selection Frame
**Rule:** Ask: will this work matter in 20 years? Not 'will it be cited' — will it have changed something about how the world works?

**Test:** Name one paper from 20 years ago that shaped this researcher's current work. Then ask: does their current work have that kind of load-bearing quality? If the comparison is uncomfortable, the long-view filter isn't being applied.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science, Ch. 1

### Work Where You Have an Unfair Advantage
**Rule:** The best problem is one where your specific background, access, or intuition gives you an edge others don't have.

**Test:** Name the specific combination of background, tools, and access that makes this researcher unusually positioned for this problem. If the answer is "anyone with a PhD in this field could do this," the unfair advantage is absent.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Bell Labs history

### Avoid the Fashionable Problem Trap
**Rule:** Fashionable problems attract the most talent and are therefore the most competitive. The best problems are neglected: important, solvable, but not yet fashionable.

**Test:** How many papers appeared on this problem in the last 12 months? A rapid increase in publication count is the fashionability signal. If the field's top conferences are full of this topic, the unfashionable edge is gone.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Ask Your Field's Elders the Hard Question
**Rule:** Ask senior researchers: 'What are the most important problems nobody is working on?' Their answers often surprise.

**Test:** Has the researcher had this conversation with at least two senior colleagues in the last year? If not, they are missing the most efficient route to the unfashionable important problem list.

**Source:** You and Your Research; NPS lectures

### Convert Failure Into a Problem Definition
**Rule:** When an approach fails, convert the failure into a better problem definition. Every failed attack narrows the solution space.

**Test:** Describe the last significant failed attempt. Did it produce a sharper problem definition? If the response to failure is "we abandoned that direction," without a revised problem statement, the failure wasn't converted.

**Source:** Bell Labs technical notes; Art of Doing Science

### The Boredom Filter
**Rule:** If a problem doesn't obsess you — if you're not thinking about it in the shower — it's probably not important enough to justify years of your life.

**Test:** Ask the researcher: when did they last think about this problem outside working hours? If the answer is "not recently," the obsession signal is absent and the problem may be wrong.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Impact Multiplier Filter
**Rule:** Prefer problems whose solution becomes infrastructure that others build on. The highest-impact problems are those whose solutions enable many other solutions.

**Test:** If this problem were solved, would other researchers in adjacent areas be immediately unblocked? Name two downstream problems that this solution would enable. If none come to mind, the impact multiplier is weak.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; Hamming codes paper, 1950

### The Rephrasing Trick
**Rule:** When a problem seems unsolvable, rephrase it. Hamming repeatedly solved problems by asking 'what is the real question here?'

**Test:** State the problem three different ways, each framing the question differently. If the researcher can only state it one way, they have not explored the problem's formulation space.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science, Ch. 3

### The Nobel Prize Test
**Rule:** Ask: if this work succeeds, could a reasonable Nobel committee consider it? The question forces honest evaluation of whether the work is genuinely foundational.

**Test:** Run the thought experiment honestly: would the result, if achieved, change a fundamental understanding of the field? If the honest answer is "no, but it fills a gap," the work is important but not foundational.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Narrow Deep vs. Wide Shallow
**Rule:** The signature of great scientists is one or two deep results requiring years of focused effort, not a broad portfolio of minor contributions.

**Test:** Count publications in the last 5 years and identify the deepest single result. If there are 20+ papers and no single result dominates, the portfolio is wide-shallow.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; You and Your Research

### Problems With High Leverage
**Rule:** Seek problems whose solution removes a fundamental constraint, eliminating entire categories of downstream problems.

**Test:** If this problem is solved, how many other currently hard problems become easy? Name three specifically. If the constraint removed is narrow, the leverage is low.

**Source:** Hamming codes paper; Art of Doing Science

### The Contrarian Problem List
**Rule:** The most important problems are often considered intractable by the mainstream. Maintain a list of problems experts call hopeless and evaluate each for hidden tractability.

**Test:** Name one problem that the mainstream considers hopeless but where the researcher sees a credible path using specific available tools. If no such problem exists in their mental list, the contrarian filter is absent.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Results Must Be Communicable
**Rule:** A result that cannot be communicated has no impact. Choose problems where the solution, if found, can be clearly explained and verified.

**Test:** Can the researcher explain the problem and its significance to a smart non-expert in 5 minutes? If the explanation requires 30 minutes of background, the communicability is too low for wide impact.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science, Ch. 4

### The Simplicity Standard
**Rule:** The most important results are almost always expressible simply. If your result requires extreme complexity to state, you may not have found the right formulation.

**Test:** State the core claim of the current work in one sentence. If it requires two sentences of qualifications before it makes sense, the formulation may not be the deepest one.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; You and Your Research

### Avoid the Thesis-Continuation Trap
**Rule:** Most researchers spend 10 years extending their PhD thesis. The thesis was a training problem; your career requires finding the real problem.

**Test:** Is the current research agenda a direct extension of the PhD thesis? If yes, and if more than 5 years have passed since the PhD, the transition to the researcher's real problem may not have occurred.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Read the History of Your Field
**Rule:** Understanding how great problems were identified and solved tells you where the next great problems are likely to come from.

**Test:** Can the researcher trace the lineage of the three most important results in their field and identify the pattern of how each was found? Missing this history is missing the field's predictive structure.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures

### The Tool-Problem Mismatch
**Rule:** Often the reason an important problem remains unsolved is that the available tools are wrong for it. Making the tool may be the real work.

**Test:** Is the barrier to solving the target problem a lack of tools rather than a lack of insight? If tool-building would unlock the problem, is the researcher willing to spend 2 years building tools before attacking the problem?

**Source:** You and Your Research; Bell Labs technical culture

### The Aesthetic Criterion
**Rule:** Great problems are often beautiful in their formulation. Train the aesthetic sense — ugly, complicated problem statements rarely lead to foundational results.

**Test:** Does the problem statement feel beautiful or clunky? This is a trained aesthetic judgment. Ask whether simplifying the framing reveals a cleaner underlying question.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science

### Follow the Anomalies
**Rule:** Anomalies — places where the current theory doesn't fit the data — are the most reliable indicator of where the next important breakthrough will come from.

**Test:** Name one anomaly in the researcher's field — a result that the current best theory doesn't cleanly explain. If none can be named, the researcher isn't monitoring where the theory is breaking down.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures

### Don't Let Fashion Define Importance
**Rule:** What gets funded and published is often determined by fashion, not importance. Judge importance by consequences, not by the field's current attention.

**Test:** Is the researcher's funding profile driven by current grant fashions or by the importance hierarchy they've independently constructed? If every funded project aligns with current funding priorities, fashion is doing the selecting.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Field-Selection Is Career-Defining
**Rule:** Your field determines the ceiling of your impact. Periodically re-evaluate whether your field has a future and whether its important problems are still unsolved.

**Test:** In the researcher's field, are the most important problems still open, or have they been substantially solved? If the best problems are solved or in progress by others, the field's ceiling may have lowered and migration is worth considering.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science, Ch. 2

## Layer 2 — Approach Taste
*Given the problem, which angle to attack from*

Hamming was obsessed with how great scientists work — not just what they worked on, but how they operated day-to-day. This layer distils his observations about style, method, and the habits that separate productive researchers from unproductive ones. The doctrine is particularly strong on communication, emotional investment, and the underrated role of open collaboration.

### Work With Your Door Open
**Rule:** Scientists who close their door get more done in the short term and less in the long term. The interruptions provide cross-pollination that produces breakthroughs.

**Test:** Is the researcher's door (literal or metaphorical) open to colleagues dropping by with problems and observations? A policy of scheduled-only interactions is the closed door pattern.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Emotional Investment Is Not Optional
**Rule:** You cannot do great work without being emotionally invested. Scientists who treat their work as 9-to-5 produce 9-to-5 results.

**Test:** Does the researcher talk about their work with visible energy and engagement? Flat, professional affect when describing the work is a signal that emotional investment is absent.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Subconscious Working
**Rule:** Deliberately load a hard problem into your mind before sleeping or before routine tasks. Many breakthroughs come during commutes, walks, or the first minutes after waking.

**Test:** Does the researcher have a habit of deliberately reviewing unsolved problems before non-work activities? If problem-solving is strictly confined to desk time, the subconscious channel is closed.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Style of the Great Scientist
**Rule:** Every great scientist has a recognizable style. Developing your own is not vanity; it's the accumulation of specific intuitions, tools, and aesthetic sensibility.

**Test:** Can colleagues describe this researcher's scientific style in one sentence — their characteristic approach or angle? If peers cannot characterize their style, it hasn't yet crystallized.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science

### Tolerate Ambiguity
**Rule:** Great scientists are comfortable sitting with ambiguity for months or years while clarity slowly emerges. Most researchers avoid this discomfort.

**Test:** Is the researcher currently holding an open, unresolved question without forcing premature closure? Comfort with ambiguity is observable: do they demand resolution quickly, or can they sit with open questions?

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Write It Up, Even Early
**Rule:** Writing forces a clarity of thought that thinking alone never achieves. Write current understanding even before results — the act reveals gaps.

**Test:** Has the researcher written up the current state of understanding on their main problem in the last 3 months, even if incomplete? If not, writing-as-thinking is not in the practice.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science, Ch. 4

### Give Talks Before You're Ready
**Rule:** The discipline of presenting half-formed ideas accelerates research. Questions from an audience reveal blind spots that don't emerge in solo work.

**Test:** Has the researcher presented current, unfinished work to an audience in the last 6 months? Waiting until "ready" to present is the closed-feedback pattern.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Learn the Style of Communication in Your Field
**Rule:** Brilliance that isn't communicated effectively is wasted. Bad writing and bad presentations have buried good science.

**Test:** Ask a non-specialist to read the researcher's last paper introduction. Can they state the contribution in one sentence? Failure to produce that sentence means the writing has not achieved the field's communication standard.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science, Ch. 4

### Prepare for Luck
**Rule:** Pasteur's maxim: chance favors the prepared mind. Luck plays a large role in great discoveries, but only prepared minds can convert it into breakthroughs.

**Test:** When the researcher describes their most recent unexpected finding, did they recognize its significance immediately, or did it take months? Fast recognition = prepared mind. Slow recognition = the seed portfolio was insufficient.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Systems Over One-Off Solutions
**Rule:** When attacking a problem, ask not just 'how do I solve this case?' but 'what general method would solve all cases of this type?'

**Test:** Does the current approach yield a general method or a specific solution? If specific, ask: can it be generalized? Hamming codes solved all single-bit error correction, not one specific circuit.

**Source:** Hamming codes paper; Digital Filters, 1977

### Use the Best Tools Available
**Rule:** Hamming was an early computer adopter precisely because better tools enable better research. Don't be sentimental about old methods.

**Test:** Is the researcher using the current state-of-the-art tools for their domain? Sentimental attachment to older methods without a performance-based justification is the tool-inertia failure.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; Bell Labs computing history

### Master the Mathematics of Your Field
**Rule:** Whatever your field, mathematics is its deepest language. Researchers who avoid the mathematics limit themselves to surface-level understanding.

**Test:** Can the researcher derive the foundational equations of their field from first principles? If core results require looking up proofs, the mathematical mastery is incomplete.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures

### The Confidence to Be Wrong
**Rule:** The fear of being wrong keeps researchers in safe territory. Great scientists make bold claims, are wrong publicly, and revise. Being cautious is not the same as being rigorous.

**Test:** Has the researcher made a bold, specific, falsifiable claim in public in the last 12 months? If all public claims are hedged to the point where they can't be wrong, caution has replaced rigor.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Read Broadly, Think Narrowly
**Rule:** The best breakthroughs often come from applying tools or insights from one field to problems in another. Broad reading requires deliberate effort.

**Test:** Name one result from a field outside the researcher's primary domain that they read in the last 6 months. If no cross-domain reading has happened, the cross-pollination channel is closed.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures

### The Three Components of Great Work
**Rule:** Three essentials: a significant problem, sustained attack over a long period, courage to push to a fundamental result rather than stopping at a preliminary finding.

**Test:** Does the current work have all three? Score explicitly: (1) is the problem significant? (2) has the attack been sustained >2 years? (3) is the team pushing to a fundamental result, not a preliminary one? Any missing component is the bottleneck.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Think Consistently About the Future
**Rule:** Devote some part of every week to where your field is going in 10-20 years. Getting ahead of the wave is a major source of competitive advantage.

**Test:** What does the researcher predict their field's most important problem will be in 15 years? If no answer is immediately available, the future-thinking discipline is absent.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures

### Collaboration as Signal-Amplifier
**Rule:** Working with smart people who know different things amplifies your signal. Hamming used Bell Labs' concentration of talent deliberately.

**Test:** Name three colleagues with different specializations that the researcher interacts with regularly on technical questions. If the collaboration network is narrow or single-domain, signal amplification is limited.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Bell Labs culture

### The Long Fuse
**Rule:** The most important work often takes years or decades to be recognized. Calibrate on long-term importance, not immediate recognition.

**Test:** Is the researcher's timeline for impact measured in years or quarters? If the expectation is recognition within 12 months, the work may be optimized for short-fuse results.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Friday Afternoon Experiment
**Rule:** Set aside time each week for undirected exploration. Some fraction of the most important Bell Labs discoveries came from undirected play.

**Test:** Does the researcher have a weekly time block specifically for undirected exploration — with no deliverable attached? If all working time is allocated to projects, play time is zero.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Bell Labs research culture

### The Error-Correcting Mind
**Rule:** Build redundancy into your thinking. Reach the same conclusion from multiple different angles. A single-path insight may be an artifact of that path.

**Test:** For the current main result, has the researcher verified it via at least two independent methods? If the result rests on a single derivation or experiment, the error-correcting redundancy is absent.

**Source:** Hamming codes paper; Art of Doing Science

### Study Your Own Thinking
**Rule:** Meta-cognition is underrated as a research skill. Understand your own cognitive patterns: when most productive, what conditions produce best insights.

**Test:** Can the researcher describe their peak cognitive conditions — time of day, environment, pre-conditions for best insight? If this self-knowledge is absent, the meta-cognitive discipline hasn't been applied.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science

### Keep a Notebook of Great Ideas
**Rule:** Maintain a running list of ideas, including half-formed ones. Revisit monthly. Many great results start as a vague sense that 'something is possible here.'

**Test:** Does the researcher have an active idea notebook — digital or physical — that they review regularly? If not, half-formed ideas are evaporating.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Attack From Multiple Angles Simultaneously
**Rule:** Launch several attack angles simultaneously. Each failed attack reveals structure about the problem. The combination often suggests the right approach.

**Test:** On the current main problem, how many distinct attack angles have been tried? If only one has been attempted, the multi-angle strategy is not in use.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Articulation Test
**Rule:** If you can't articulate why a problem is important to a smart non-expert in five minutes, you don't understand it deeply enough.

**Test:** Give the researcher 5 minutes to explain to a smart non-expert why their problem matters. Failure to achieve a convincing explanation in that time is a signal that the importance hasn't been internalized at the deepest level.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science

### Maintain the Beginner's Eye
**Rule:** Experts miss solutions obvious to beginners because they carry the baggage of knowing what 'should' work. Challenge unstated assumptions periodically.

**Test:** List three assumptions the field treats as fixed that the researcher has personally questioned. If no assumptions have been challenged in the last year, the beginner's eye is closed.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures

### The Work Compounds
**Rule:** Each year of work makes the next more productive. This compounding requires working on related problems over a long period — too many pivots prevent it.

**Test:** Has the researcher's body of work built cumulatively, with each project informed by the last? If the research history shows unrelated pivots, the compounding hasn't been maintained.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science

### Solve the Meta-Problem
**Rule:** Sometimes the most important work is building infrastructure — methods, languages, tools — that allows many people to solve object-level problems.

**Test:** Could the researcher's current work be reframed as infrastructure that unlocks 100 other researchers? If yes and they haven't framed it that way, a high-leverage reframe is available.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; Turing Award lecture, 1968

### Manage Your Ambition Carefully
**Rule:** Too little ambition produces safe work. Too much produces paralysis. Right calibration: the most important problem where you have a plausible path in 3-5 years.

**Test:** Is the current problem at the intersection of "most important I can envision" and "plausible path in 3-5 years"? If it's easier than the most important they can imagine, ambition is undersized. If there's no plausible path, the ambition is uncoupled from tractability.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Persistence on the Right Problem
**Rule:** There is a crucial difference between persistence (continuing to attack an important problem) and stubbornness (continuing a failed approach). Persist on the problem; abandon the approach readily.

**Test:** In the last year, how many distinct approaches to the same core problem have been tried? If only one approach has been used over 12+ months without success, stubbornness may have replaced persistence.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

## Layer 3 — Stopping Taste
*When to abandon, when to push, when "done enough"*

Hamming had precise views on when to stop. The core insight: stopping is a problem-selection decision in reverse. The same criteria that determine whether to start a problem also determine whether to continue it.

### The 'So What' Test
**Rule:** Before writing the paper, ask 'so what?' What does this result enable? What does it generalize to? If answers are 'not much,' the result may need extension.

**Test:** State two things this result enables that were not possible before. If the best answer is "it adds to the literature," the so-what test has failed and the result needs generalization before publication.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Stop When the Field Has Passed You
**Rule:** Signs: defending your results against the new generation rather than building on them; methods being taught in textbooks. Time to change direction.

**Test:** Is the researcher's primary activity building on their results, or defending them? More than one public defense in a year against new approaches is the field-moving-on signal.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Know When to Change Fields
**Rule:** Hamming changed fields at least three times. Each change was driven by the same logic: important problems here are solved or blocked; that field's are accessible to me.

**Test:** In the researcher's current field, are the most important remaining problems blocked (by politics, funding, or solved-not-yet-published)? If yes, and an adjacent field has open important problems accessible to this researcher's skills, field migration is warranted.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Hamming's career history

### Stop When You've Solved the General Problem
**Rule:** Know when a result is 'done' — the general principle is established and the field can take it from here. Diminishing returns on refinement are real.

**Test:** Is the current work refining a result that is already established in general principle? If the core insight is sound and the remaining work is constant-factor improvements, the general problem is solved. Stop.

**Source:** Hamming codes paper, 1950

### The Failed-Three-Attacks Rule
**Rule:** Three fundamentally different attacks all failed → stop temporarily. Set it aside for 3-6 months. The subconscious needs distance.

**Test:** Count the number of fundamentally different attack strategies that have been tried on the current problem. Three failures of genuinely different approaches is the threshold for a deliberate pause.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Hamming's problem-switching pattern

### The Diminishing-Returns Threshold
**Rule:** Distinguish diminishing returns on the current approach (switch approaches) from diminishing returns on the problem itself (switch problems).

**Test:** Is progress slowing because the current approach is exhausted, or because the problem itself is yielding less? Diagnose explicitly before either switching approaches or switching problems.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### Abandon Preliminaries That Have Become Ends
**Rule:** When the stepping stone has become the destination without reaching the destination, the project may have lost its purpose.

**Test:** What was the original goal that this preliminary work was meant to enable? Is the team still working toward that goal, or has the preliminary become the endpoint? If the original goal has been forgotten, the mission has drifted.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Recognition Trap
**Rule:** Fame changes what researchers work on, almost always for the worse. The temptation is to defend reputation by working on safe problems. Watch for this drift.

**Test:** Has the researcher's choice of problems become safer or more conventional since achieving recognition? Compare their current problem portfolio to what they worked on before becoming well-known.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Management Transition
**Rule:** Management should be a deliberate choice, not a default promotion. Taking the management path should be consciously aligned with your goals.

**Test:** If offered a management promotion, does the researcher have a clear answer about whether management or individual research better serves their long-term contribution goals? Accepting management as a default is the trap.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Bell Labs promotion culture

### The Last Paper Test
**Rule:** Ask: if I wrote one more paper and then stopped, what should it be? If you're not actively working on that paper, ask why.

**Test:** Can the researcher state the most important paper they haven't written yet? And are they currently working on it? Inability to name it or working on something else instead is the signal.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### When a Field Is Mined Out
**Rule:** The best people are leaving for adjacent fields. When the best people leave, follow them. Their revealed preferences are data about where the frontier has moved.

**Test:** Are the best researchers the researcher most respects still working in this field, or have they moved to adjacent areas? Tracking the migration pattern of the top 5 researchers they most respect is the signal.

**Source:** You and Your Research; Hamming's migration to computer science

### The Age-and-Stage Calibration
**Rule:** Early career: develop taste and tools. Mid-career: do the most important work you're capable of. Late career: teach, communicate, and identify the next generation's important problems.

**Test:** Does the researcher's current allocation (between doing versus teaching versus identifying future problems) match their career stage? Mid-career researchers spending most time teaching are potentially misallocated.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures

### Stop Optimizing for Output, Start Optimizing for Legacy
**Rule:** At some point the right metric shifts from 'how many papers this year' to 'what will I leave behind that matters.'

**Test:** Is the researcher still measuring success primarily by publication count or grant dollars? At mid-to-late career, switching the primary metric to long-term impact is the right transition.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures

### When to Publish vs. When to Continue
**Rule:** Publish when: central claim is established, checked via two independent routes, most important implications are worked out. If any one is missing, continue.

**Test:** Run the three-part checklist: (1) central claim established? (2) verified via two independent routes? (3) main implications worked out? Publishing without all three is premature.

**Source:** Art of Doing Science; Bell Labs publication culture

### Don't Over-Defend
**Rule:** A correct result will ultimately be recognized. Over-defending is a distraction. Write one rebuttal if necessary, then prove the critics wrong with better work.

**Test:** Has the researcher written more than one public response to a single line of criticism? If yes, the over-defense pattern may have activated.

**Source:** You and Your Research, 1986

### The Generalization Imperative Before Stopping
**Rule:** Before stopping on a result, ask: what is the most general form of this? Always attempt the generalization before declaring the work done.

**Test:** State the most general form of the current result. If the current result handles only specific cases, the generalization attempt is the next mandatory step before closing.

**Source:** Hamming codes paper; Art of Doing Science

### Exit Status Fields
**Rule:** When a field rewards prestige proxies over important results, the best work moves elsewhere. Know the ecology of your field and migrate accordingly.

**Test:** In the researcher's field, are grants and prestigious publications primarily rewarded for importance or for prestige-proxy signals (venue, affiliation, topic fashionability)? If prestige proxy dominates, the field's ecology has degraded and migration is warranted.

**Source:** You and Your Research; NPS lectures

## 2026 Overlay

### Where the alpha is
- AI-assisted scientific discovery: problems that were computationally intractable are now tractable — high-value targets
- Biology and materials science: the 'atoms' equivalent of the software revolution, now accessible
- Fundamental mathematics with real-world consequences: the Hamming-Shannon legacy suggests underinvestment here
- Tools for science: better research infrastructure compounds for the entire field
- Cross-disciplinary problems at the intersection of ML and physical science

### Auto-reject
- Research that optimizes for publication count over importance
- Fields where the primary innovation is in applying existing ML methods rather than in the science itself
- Problems that require regulatory capture or political consensus to matter
- Work that is important only within a narrow sub-field with no generalization potential
- Research programs driven by funding availability rather than problem importance

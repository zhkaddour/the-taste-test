# The Graham Doctrine
### A Doctrine of Paul Graham

> *"Live in the future, then build what's missing."*

**Created:** 2026-05-02 · **Layers:** 3 · **Doctrines:** 94

---

**About this playbook:** A three-layer filter for evaluating startup ideas distilled from Paul Graham's essay corpus on paulgraham.com (2001-2025), Hackers & Painters (2004), the Y Combinator playbook and Startup School talks, the YC partner-letter mechanism, the Viaweb operating record, 'Founder Mode' (2024), and the Twitter/X corpus through 2026. Where Thiel hunts monopolies via secrets and Andreessen hunts waves via PMF, Graham hunts organic ideas via lived users — small, painful, repulsive on the surface — and runs the company on growth rate and default-alive arithmetic.

---

## Layer 01 — PROBLEM
### Problem-Selection Taste
*What is worth working on at all*

Graham's filter is the inverse of consultancy strategy: don't sit down to think of a startup idea, live at the edge of the future and notice what's missing. Real ideas are organic, often repulsive on the surface, and almost always look small. The reason smart people miss them isn't that the ideas are hidden — it's that the ideas are unconsciously filtered out as too unglamorous, too hard, or too obviously not a 'big startup.'

---

**01.01 · Live in the Future, Then Build What's Missing** `Essays`

**Rule:** The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of them. Move yourself to the edge of where the world is going (technology, demographic, behavior) and look back at the present for things conspicuously absent.

**Test:** Can the founder name two things that obviously will exist in 5 years that don't exist now — and is one of them what they're building? If they're inventing the idea from scratch in a brainstorm, the idea is hollow.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Get Startup Ideas (Nov 2012)*

---

**01.02 · Organic Ideas Beat Invented Ideas** `Essays`

**Rule:** The best ideas are noticed, not invented. They feel like a gap the founder is annoyed exists. Invented ideas — generated from market-mapping, brainstorms, or 'what's hot' surveys — almost always die.

**Test:** Did this idea originate from a personal need or a side project, or from a strategy session? If the latter, expect the cooling-off failure within 12 months.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Get Startup Ideas (Nov 2012)*

---

**01.03 · Schlep Blindness** `Essays`

**Rule:** The best ideas are repulsive on the surface — they involve schleps (hassle, regulators, sales calls, support tickets, hardware, payment rails) that smart founders unconsciously filter out. The schlep is the moat.

**Test:** Does the idea make the founder slightly groan when they describe the operational reality? If yes, that's the signal it's underfunded; if no, it's probably crowded.

*Source: PG Essay: Schlep Blindness (Jan 2012)*

---

**01.04 · Frighteningly Ambitious, Pragmatically Wedged** `Essays`

**Rule:** The most ambitious ideas (search, social, payments, an Apple replacement) are too scary to attack head-on. The trick is wedging into them via a small, ridiculous-looking starting product that nonetheless lives inside the bigger arc.

**Test:** What is the embarrassingly small wedge, and what is the frighteningly ambitious endgame? If only one is articulated, the founder is either delusional or unambitious.

*Source: PG Essay: Frighteningly Ambitious Startup Ideas (Mar 2012)*

---

**01.05 · Make Something People Want** `YC`

**Rule:** The YC motto. The first test of any idea is not whether it has a moat, a market size, or a brand — it's whether someone would actively want it.

**Test:** Have you put a v0 in front of a real user? Did they ask for it again, or were they polite? If polite, you don't have a startup yet.

*Source: PG Essay: Be Good (Apr 2008); YC motto*

---

**01.06 · Start With a Specific User** `Essays`

**Rule:** Have a specific person — not a persona, an actual human you can text — in mind for v0. Vague target users produce vague products that please nobody.

**Test:** Name your first user by first name. Have you texted them this week? If you cannot name them, your problem statement is a marketing fiction.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups (Oct 2006), #10*

---

**01.07 · Beating the Averages — The Tool Advantage** `Essays`

**Rule:** Choose tools and abstractions that other people aren't using yet. Lisp at Viaweb let two people build what Yahoo Store later struggled to maintain with dozens. The 'blub paradox' — programmers can't see above their own language — is real for languages, frameworks, and AI capabilities.

**Test:** Are you building with the most powerful stack available, or with whatever's safe and conventional? If conventional, you're choosing to compete on labor instead of leverage.

*Source: PG Essay: Beating the Averages (Apr 2003)*

---

**01.08 · Hackers and Painters Frame** `HnP`

**Rule:** Programmers are makers, not scientists or engineers. Treat startup-building as a creative discipline — taste, iteration, finished works — rather than a planning discipline.

**Test:** Is the founder more energized by 'shipping the next version' or by 'completing the spec'? Only the first is a maker; only the maker survives the trough.

*Source: PG Essay: Hackers and Painters (May 2003); Hackers & Painters book, 2004*

---

**01.09 · What Doesn't Seem Like Work** `Essays`

**Rule:** If a kind of work feels like work to you, you'll be outcompeted by someone for whom it doesn't. Pick problems where your fascination is unforced — that's the long-cycle energy supply.

**Test:** What did you do this past Sunday afternoon, voluntarily? If you cannot connect that to the company, you may be picking against your own grain.

*Source: PG Essay: What Doesn't Seem Like Work (Jan 2010)*

---

**01.10 · Cities and Ambition** `Essays`

**Rule:** Cities and scenes broadcast a message about what to be ambitious about. Place yourself in a scene whose message is 'build something the world will use,' not 'be respectable' or 'be rich.'

**Test:** Walk through your daily social environment. Does the dominant message reinforce founder ambition? If you're surrounded by professional-prestige signals, your conviction is being sandblasted.

*Source: PG Essay: Cities and Ambition (May 2008)*

---

**01.11 · The Lesson to Unlearn — School Is Test-Hacking** `Essays`

**Rule:** School trains you to optimize for tests, not to figure out what's true. The founder must consciously unlearn this — startups are graded by reality, and reality has no rubric.

**Test:** When facing ambiguity, does the founder ask 'what's the right answer here?' (test-hacking) or 'what's actually true?' (truth-seeking)? Only the second survives the next pivot.

*Source: PG Essay: The Lesson to Unlearn (Dec 2019)*

---

**01.12 · The Hardware Renaissance Door** `Essays`

**Rule:** Hardware startups were once nearly impossible; cheap prototyping, contract manufacturing, and crowdfunding reopened the door. Bias toward physical-world problems whose technical barrier was about-to-fall when you arrived.

**Test:** Was the technical/economic barrier to this product impossible 5 years ago and trivial today? If yes, you arrived at the right moment; if it's been possible for a decade, ask why no one already won.

*Source: PG Essay: The Hardware Renaissance (Oct 2012)*

---

**01.13 · Black Swan Farming** `Essays`

**Rule:** Startup outcomes follow a power law dominated by 1-2 companies per batch. Most companies that look great fail; a few that look weird become enormous. Plant for outliers, not for averages.

**Test:** If this idea succeeds beyond the founder's stated dreams, what is the outcome? If the answer is a $50M business, it's a small business — not a startup.

*Source: PG Essay: Black Swan Farming (Sep 2012)*

---

**01.14 · Mean People Fail** `Essays`

**Rule:** Mean people, in startups especially, lose. Meanness selects for the wrong allies, the wrong customers, and the wrong information environment. Selecting good people upfront is selecting against meanness.

**Test:** Watch how the founder treats the waiter, the contractor, and the candidate they aren't going to hire. Meanness in low-stakes interactions predicts toxic governance later.

*Source: PG Essay: Mean People Fail (Nov 2014)*

---

**01.15 · Disconnecting Distraction** `Essays`

**Rule:** The hidden cost of always-on internet is the inability to do real work. Founders who can't disconnect lose their depth, and depth is where novel insight comes from.

**Test:** Has the founder built any deliberate structure — phone-off blocks, no-meeting days, deep-work environments — that protects multi-hour focus? If not, the company is operating on shallow throughput.

*Source: PG Essay: Disconnecting Distraction (May 2008)*

---

**01.16 · Why to Not Not Start a Startup** `Essays`

**Rule:** Most reasons people give themselves for not starting a startup are rationalizations of fear. Itemize them, and most evaporate when stated plainly.

**Test:** List the founder's top three fears about starting. Are they technical/market risks, or self-image risks? The latter are the dominant kill, and they should be confronted, not respected.

*Source: PG Essay: Why to Not Not Start a Startup (Mar 2007)*

---

**01.17 · The Nerd-Index Heuristic** `Essays`

**Rule:** When the user is a developer, founder, or other nerd, the product can be technically sophisticated and minimally explained. When the user is general, every assumption must be re-examined against non-technical reality.

**Test:** Who is the user? If 'developers,' assume self-discovery; if 'small business owner,' assume nothing — every step must be designed.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Start a Startup (Mar 2005); YC interviews*

---

**01.18 · Wave-Plus-Seed: Wedge Then Wave** `Essays`

**Rule:** Founders should ride a wave (a real underlying technology shift) AND start with a seed product narrow enough to be lovable. Wave alone = drift; wedge alone = no growth ceiling.

**Test:** What is the wave (e.g., LLM capability, mobile, hardware costs) and what is the seed product? If you cannot name both crisply, you have either a slogan or a feature.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Get Startup Ideas (Nov 2012); Startup School talks*

---

**01.19 · Boring Adjacent Markets** `Essays`

**Rule:** The best ideas often look unsexy from outside the niche but obvious from inside it. Glamour is anti-correlated with PMF — glamorous markets are crowded, boring ones are owned.

**Test:** Does describing the company at a dinner party produce polite confusion or excitement? Polite confusion is often the right signal.

*Source: PG Essay: Schlep Blindness (Jan 2012); Startup School 2014*

---

**01.20 · B2B Pain Beats B2C Delight** `YC`

**Rule:** B2B users have measurable, recurring pain and budgets. B2C users have moods. Default toward problems where the user is paid to care.

**Test:** Will the customer's boss notice if they cancel the product? If yes, you have B2B retention shape. If only the customer themselves notices, you have a hits business.

*Source: Startup School talks, 2009-2014; YC partner letters*

---

**01.21 · Avoid Marginal Niches** `Essays`

**Rule:** Choosing too-narrow a market for fear of competition usually fails. Niches must be small enough to dominate but large enough that domination is meaningful.

**Test:** If you owned 100% of this niche, would the company be venture-fundable? If no, you've optimized away from the prize.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups (Oct 2006), #3*

---

**01.22 · Avoid Derivative Ideas** `Essays`

**Rule:** Pure clones of existing winners almost never beat the original. If the only difference is geography or color scheme, you don't have an idea.

**Test:** Strip the cosmetic differences. Is the underlying mechanic novel, or are you a 'Foo for X' version of someone else? Foo-for-X usually fails.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups (Oct 2006), #4*

---

**01.23 · Don't Pick Bad Locations** `Essays`

**Rule:** Cities and scenes have radically different startup densities and norms. The default for an ambitious founder is to move to where the talent and capital already are, not to wait for them to come.

**Test:** Can the founder hire a 5th engineer locally in 8 weeks at market rate? If no, the location is structurally wrong for this stage.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #2; Cities and Ambition (May 2008)*

---

**01.24 · The Idea Maze** `YC`

**Rule:** Every promising startup idea is a maze: many paths look similar from the outside, but only a few lead to gold. The founder's edge is having walked enough of the maze to know which dead ends to skip.

**Test:** Can the founder enumerate 3-5 ways the idea could be tried, and articulate why each fails — except the one they're pursuing? If not, they haven't earned conviction.

*Source: Startup School 2013 (PG and Balaji)*

---

**01.25 · Talk to Users to Find the Idea** `Essays`

**Rule:** Even before there's a product, talking to potential users — shadowing them, watching what they do — is the single highest-yield activity. Most teams skip it because it feels like not-yet-real-work.

**Test:** How many users has the founder talked to in the last 30 days? Below 10 in pre-PMF, the team is operating on guesses.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Start a Startup (Mar 2005); Do Things That Don't Scale (Jul 2013)*

---

**01.26 · Look Where Smart People Are Slumming** `YC`

**Rule:** When unusually capable people are taking unusual jobs (PhDs going into trade journalism, MIT grads installing batteries), the surrounding domain is about to undergo a phase change. Fund the phase change.

**Test:** Are A-tier people in the founder's network voluntarily moving toward this domain? Their migration is leading-edge data.

*Source: Startup School / YC talks, 2009-2014*

---

**01.27 · Two-Founder Default** `Essays`

**Rule:** Solo founders fail at higher rates because of morale, blind spots, and lack of decisional sounding-board. Two technical co-founders bonded by years of trust is the strongest pattern.

**Test:** Is the founder solo? If yes, the burden of proof is materially higher; if not, do the co-founders have ≥3 years of working trust before this venture?

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #1*

---

**01.28 · Maker Schedule, Manager Schedule** `Essays`

**Rule:** Makers (programmers, writers, designers) need 4-hour blocks to do their work. Managers operate in 1-hour increments. Companies that don't separate these schedules destroy maker output.

**Test:** Do the founders have at least 2 maker-shaped days per week with no scheduled meetings? If every day is calendar-fragmented, the company is run on manager-time even when the work is maker-shaped.

*Source: PG Essay: Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule (Jul 2009)*

---

**01.29 · Avoid the Marginal Investor Refuge** `Essays`

**Rule:** Going to consultants, banks, or law firms 'until I figure out my idea' is a slow exit ramp from ever starting. Optionality has a half-life.

**Test:** How long has the founder been in 'optionality jobs' since deciding they want to build? Above 18 months, the muscle is atrophying — start now or accept they won't.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Lose Time and Money (Jul 2010)*

---

**01.30 · Anti-Big-Idea Bias** `YC`

**Rule:** When everyone agrees the big idea is X, the alpha is gone — go work on the unconfident-but-loved small thing instead. The big-idea consensus is usually 6 months behind the front line.

**Test:** Is the idea exciting in a way that makes pitch listeners light up, or in a way that makes them slightly puzzled? The second is more often a real signal.

*Source: Startup School talks 2010-2014*

---

**01.31 · The Frontier-of-Tools Test** `Essays`

**Rule:** An idea is more likely to be load-bearing if it can only be built using tools or capabilities that became available recently. If it could have been built five years ago — and wasn't — ask why.

**Test:** Which capability launched in the last 24-36 months makes this idea newly possible? If you cannot name one, you may be working on a known dead-end.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Get Startup Ideas (Nov 2012)*

---

**01.32 · Unimportant Important Problem Filter** `Essays`

**Rule:** Some problems are important and obviously hard (cold fusion); some are unimportant. The sweet spot for startups is problems that look unimportant from a distance but are deeply important once you understand them.

**Test:** Does the public framing of the problem make it sound trivial? If yes, and the founder can articulate why it's actually important, the under-appreciation is alpha.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Get Startup Ideas (Nov 2012); Schlep Blindness*

---

## Layer 02 — APPROACH
### Approach Taste
*Given the problem, which angle to attack from*

Once the idea is real, the operating playbook is unromantic and very specific: do things that don't scale, optimize the master metric (weekly growth), be default-alive, talk to users daily, hire only when you must, raise enough to keep going, and stay in founder mode. Graham's mechanism is iteration speed plus character (relentless resourcefulness, formidability, determination) — not master plans or moats.

---

**02.01 · Do Things That Don't Scale** `Essays`

**Rule:** Hand-recruit your first users. Do 'manual delivery' for them. Provide unscalable, one-of-a-kind concierge service to the first 100. The reason this works is precisely because competitors won't.

**Test:** What unscalable thing did the founders personally do for users this week? If 'we built features and ran ads,' they're skipping the highest-EV phase.

*Source: PG Essay: Do Things That Don't Scale (Jul 2013)*

---

**02.02 · The Collison Installation** `Essays`

**Rule:** When users say they'd use the product, sit next to them and install it with them on their machine — name the install-conversion difference. This is the early-Stripe pattern.

**Test:** Have the founders done a Collison installation this week? If not, signed-up but inactive users are eating the company silently.

*Source: PG Essay: Do Things That Don't Scale (Jul 2013)*

---

**02.03 · Manual Delivery Until Automation Is Earned** `Essays`

**Rule:** Run the operation manually long enough that you actually understand the workflow. Premature automation builds the wrong abstractions and locks in the wrong product.

**Test:** Are the founders still doing parts of the operation by hand to learn? If everything is already automated pre-PMF, the team has skipped the learning phase.

*Source: PG Essay: Do Things That Don't Scale (Jul 2013)*

---

**02.04 · Startup = Growth** `Essays`

**Rule:** What separates a startup from a small business is growth rate. Everything else is downstream of the growth-rate question. A startup is, definitionally, the kind of company designed to grow fast.

**Test:** What is the trailing 8-week weekly growth rate? If it's not the master metric, the team is optimizing the wrong loop.

*Source: PG Essay: Startup = Growth (Sep 2012)*

---

**02.05 · The 5-7% Weekly Growth Bar** `Essays`

**Rule:** 5-7% week-over-week is the YC bar. Sustained, that's the difference between a startup and a hobby. Below 1% w/w sustained, the company is definitionally not yet a startup.

**Test:** Calibrate to the growth bar — over a 12-week trailing window. If sub-1% sustained, you have not yet found the wave; pivot or kill.

*Source: PG Essay: Startup = Growth (Sep 2012)*

---

**02.06 · Default Alive Calculation** `Essays`

**Rule:** Compute, with current cash, current burn, and current growth rate, whether the company gets to profitability before the cash runs out. The answer is binary: default alive or default dead.

**Test:** What's your default-alive date today? If the founder cannot answer in seconds, they are running on hope rather than arithmetic.

*Source: PG Essay: Default Alive or Default Dead (Oct 2015)*

---

**02.07 · Don't Raise Too Little** `Essays`

**Rule:** Underraising kills companies that would have worked. The wrong place to be cheap is on the runway that buys you the chance to find PMF.

**Test:** Do you have ≥15-18 months of runway at current burn? Below 12, you'll be raising again before you've learned what to raise on.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #11*

---

**02.08 · Don't Spend Too Much** `Essays`

**Rule:** Most companies that die from overspending die because they hired too fast. Hiring is the largest line item and the hardest to reverse.

**Test:** Are you adding headcount before adding users that need them? If hiring is leading rather than lagging usage, you're burning future runway on present politics.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #12*

---

**02.09 · Don't Raise Too Much** `Essays`

**Rule:** Raising 'too much' invites premature scale, exec-tier hiring, and process — none of which a pre-PMF company can support. Optimal raise is the smallest amount that buys ~18 months at honest burn.

**Test:** Is the post-money 4x your defensible 12-month plan? If yes, you're guaranteeing the next round will be a down round and the team will inflate against PMF discipline.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #13*

---

**02.10 · Be Formidable, Not Persuasive** `Essays`

**Rule:** The way to convince investors is to be formidable — to have actually understood your space and have a real product working — not to perfect the pitch deck. Investors fund people they cannot dismiss.

**Test:** If you stripped the deck and just had the founder talk for 5 minutes, would the investor be impressed? If the deck is doing the lifting, formidability is missing.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Convince Investors (Aug 2013)*

---

**02.11 · Relentlessly Resourceful** `Essays`

**Rule:** The single best descriptor of a successful founder is 'relentlessly resourceful' — when blocked, they find a way around. Not just hardworking, not just smart, but unwilling to take 'no.'

**Test:** Watch how the founder handles a blocker (legal, technical, customer). Do they route around it the same week, or wait? The first is the trait.

*Source: PG Essay: Relentlessly Resourceful (Mar 2009); A Word to the Resourceful (Jan 2012)*

---

**02.12 · The Anatomy of Determination** `Essays`

**Rule:** Determination is not 'never give up' as a slogan — it is willfulness × ambition × discipline × resilience. Each subcomponent is testable; some founders look determined but lack one component and fail predictably.

**Test:** Score the founder 1-5 on each: willfulness, ambition, discipline, resilience. Below 3 on any single axis, expect failure mode in that direction.

*Source: PG Essay: The Anatomy of Determination (Sep 2009)*

---

**02.13 · Founder Mode** `FounderMode`

**Rule:** Operate in founder mode — skip-level engagement, no fake hierarchy of trust, refusal of the MBA 'manage your managers and never go around them' template. Most great companies are run in founder mode well past the size at which the manager-mode dogma says they shouldn't be.

**Test:** Does the CEO regularly go 1-2 levels deep in the org for ground truth? Or is the org explicitly structured to prevent that? If the latter, the company is running on filtered information.

*Source: PG Essay: Founder Mode (Sep 2024)*

---

**02.14 · Talk to Users Every Week** `Essays`

**Rule:** There is no cleaner forcing function for product quality. Every week, the founder talks to users — discovery interviews, support escalations, first-installs. The day they stop is the day the company starts drifting.

**Test:** How many users did each founder talk to last week? Below 5 in pre-PMF, the company is operating on guesses again.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Start a Startup (Mar 2005); Do Things That Don't Scale (Jul 2013)*

---

**02.15 · Launch Fast** `Essays`

**Rule:** Launching too late is one of the 18 mistakes — but launching too early without a specific user is also a mistake. The right shape is: launch the smallest version that delivers real value to one named user, then iterate.

**Test:** How long until first user installs v0? If >3 months in software, the team is over-building.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #8 and #9*

---

**02.16 · How to Make Wealth — Leverage and Measurement** `HnP`

**Rule:** Wealth is created when you have measurement (your work is visibly distinguishable) and leverage (a small change in your work produces a large change in outcomes). Startups exist because they offer both.

**Test:** Can each founder's contribution be measured weekly? Can their best week 10x the company? If both yes, the structure is wealth-creating; if either no, it's a salary in disguise.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Make Wealth (Apr 2004); Hackers & Painters book, 2004*

---

**02.17 · Hire Reluctantly** `Essays`

**Rule:** Each new hire is a multiplier of communication overhead and culture dilution. Default to not hiring; hire only when the marginal user-pain demands it.

**Test:** What is the specific user-facing pain that the next hire eliminates? If the answer is 'we'll figure it out,' the hire is premature.

*Source: PG Essay: Hiring is Obsolete (May 2005); Founder Mode (Sep 2024)*

---

**02.18 · Hire Programmers, Not Resumes** `HnP`

**Rule:** Look at code, not credentials. The best programmers are often the ones not the loudest in interviews — they're recognizable by what they've built.

**Test:** Have you read the candidate's code? If you're hiring on resume + interview alone, you're sampling style, not substance.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #6; Hackers & Painters book*

---

**02.19 · Choose the Right Platform** `Essays`

**Rule:** Building on the wrong technical platform — the Microsoft stack in 2002, AppEngine in 2014, the wrong cloud in 2010 — taxes everything you do. Tool choice is strategic.

**Test:** Is the platform choice actively helping or actively taxing the team? Neutral is rare — most platform choices either compound advantage or compound debt.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #7; Beating the Averages*

---

**02.20 · Manage Investors, Don't Be Managed** `Essays`

**Rule:** Investor management is a real skill: regular updates, asks-as-asks, decisions framed as decisions. Founders who let investors set the agenda end up running someone else's startup.

**Test:** Who set the agenda of your last investor meeting? If they did, you're being managed. If you did, you're managing.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #14*

---

**02.21 · Don't Sacrifice Users for Profits** `Essays`

**Rule:** Pre-PMF, optimizing too aggressively for revenue often degrades the product and slows the user-pull loop. Make something users love first; extract second.

**Test:** Has the company introduced friction (paywalls, ads, upsells) in the last 6 months? If usage dipped and you kept the friction, you're trading future growth for present revenue.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #15*

---

**02.22 · Get Your Hands Dirty** `Essays`

**Rule:** Founders who refuse to do the unglamorous work — customer support, recruiting, legal, billing — let those functions calcify into bureaucratic black holes early.

**Test:** Has the CEO answered customer support tickets in the last 30 days? If never, the user-experience signal is now arriving second-hand.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #16; Founder Mode 2024*

---

**02.23 · Resolve Co-Founder Fights Fast** `Essays`

**Rule:** Unresolved co-founder conflict is the #1 silent killer. Address conflicts directly within days; don't let them ferment.

**Test:** Have the co-founders had a real, hard conversation in the last 30 days? If everything is suspiciously polite, conflict is being kicked down the road.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #17*

---

**02.24 · No Half-Hearted Effort** `Essays`

**Rule:** Part-time founders, side-project founders, and 'I'll quit my job after raising' founders almost always lose to full-commitment founders. The slope of effort matters more than the level of talent.

**Test:** Is the founder operating at >70 hours/week (including thinking time, not just chair time)? Below ~50 sustained, the operating energy isn't venture-shaped.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes (Oct 2006), #18*

---

**02.25 · Be Good — and Don't Be Evil Is Redundant** `Essays`

**Rule:** Make something people want and don't be evil — Graham notes 'don't be evil' would be redundant if 'make something people want' were taken seriously. Long-arc success requires actually serving users, not extracting from them.

**Test:** If your business model collapses without dark patterns or coercive defaults, you're not running a Make-Something-People-Want company. You're running a tax.

*Source: PG Essay: Be Good (Apr 2008)*

---

**02.26 · The Right Kind of Stubborn** `Essays`

**Rule:** Successful founders are stubborn — but stubborn about goals, not methods. Stubbornness on the wrong axis (the specific feature, the specific design, the specific market segment) is a kill signal.

**Test:** When confronted with disconfirming data, does the founder change the method or the goal? Method-change = healthy stubborn; goal-change-on-data = healthy update; method-stickiness despite data = pathological.

*Source: PG Essay: The Right Kind of Stubborn (Nov 2024)*

---

**02.27 · Two Kinds of Judgment** `Essays`

**Rule:** There's judgment about people and judgment about ideas. Founders need both, but they're orthogonal — strong on one and weak on the other is a common failure mode.

**Test:** Does the founder make consistently good calls on hires AND on product decisions? If only one, design the org so the weak axis is buttressed by the co-founder.

*Source: PG Essay: Two Kinds of Judgment (Apr 2010)*

---

**02.28 · How to Disagree — DH7 Hierarchy** `Essays`

**Rule:** Argument quality has levels: name-calling (DH0) up to refuting the central point (DH6) and explicit central-point refutation (DH7). Founders who can't operate at DH5+ in their own team will be slowly out-decisioned.

**Test:** When the team disagrees, are they engaging the central point or the surface? Listening for DH-level in standup is a leading culture metric.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Disagree (Mar 2008)*

---

**02.29 · Microsoft Is Dead — Notice Platform Shifts** `Essays`

**Rule:** Be willing to call platform shifts when the data is in your hand. Microsoft in 2007, the open web in 2014, mobile in 2010, AI in 2022 — recognize the regime change before consensus does.

**Test:** When was the last time the founder publicly said 'X is dead' or 'Y is the new platform'? If never, they may be reading consensus instead of leading it.

*Source: PG Essay: Microsoft Is Dead (Apr 2007)*

---

**02.30 · Recruiting Is the Job** `Essays`

**Rule:** Recruiting top people is not an HR function — it's the founder's job for at least the first 50 hires. The hours spent are inversely correlated with later organizational pain.

**Test:** How many hours per week does the founder spend on direct recruiting outreach? Below 10 in pre-PMF and below 5 post-PMF for senior roles, the bench is being underbuilt.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Start a Startup (Mar 2005); Founder Mode 2024*

---

**02.31 · Pick the Right Co-Founders** `Essays`

**Rule:** The most important decision is who you do this with. Co-founders should be people you've already worked with under stress — not people you met at a hackathon last weekend.

**Test:** How long have the co-founders worked together at high stakes prior to this venture? Under 12 months → high blowup risk; over 3 years → typical durable pattern.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Start a Startup (Mar 2005); 18 Mistakes #1*

---

**02.32 · Charge for the Product** `YC`

**Rule:** Free products distort signal. The moment you charge, you learn whether anyone actually values it. Pre-revenue 'engagement' is half-data.

**Test:** Is anyone paying real money? If not, the love signal is unreliable; charge something — even token — and see who stays.

*Source: Startup School / YC office hours, 2008-2014*

---

**02.33 · The YC Update Cadence** `YC`

**Rule:** The discipline of writing weekly updates — what shipped, what's next, where stuck — keeps the team honest. The act of writing forces clarity that meetings hide.

**Test:** Does the company send a weekly written update to itself or its investors? If not, the team is hiding from its own week-over-week progress.

*Source: YC partner-letter mechanism, 2005-present*

---

**02.34 · Investor Herd Dynamics** `YC`

**Rule:** Investor decisions are reflexive — once one credible firm commits, the round closes fast; until then, every investor is waiting for the others. The art is producing the first commitment.

**Test:** Have you secured one anchor investor before broad fundraising? If not, you'll exhaust the fundraise on the chicken-and-egg dynamic.

*Source: Startup School: How to Raise Money; YC partner letters*

---

**02.35 · Ramen Profitable** `Essays`

**Rule:** A startup that can pay the founders enough to live on, however austerely, has bought infinite optionality. Ramen-profitable beats raising-the-next-round on every dimension that matters.

**Test:** Could the company, if it had to, cut to ramen-profitable within 60 days? If not, its dependence on capital markets is structural, not temporary.

*Source: PG Essay: Ramen Profitable (Jul 2009)*

---

**02.36 · How to Not Die** `Essays`

**Rule:** The first rule is: don't die. Growth, fundraising, recruiting all matter — but the company surviving another month dwarfs all of them. Most startup death is preventable if the founders simply refuse to stop.

**Test:** Is the team operating with the conviction that this company will absolutely not die — or are they hedging? Hedging is the start of dying.

*Source: PG Essay: How Not to Die (Aug 2007)*

## Layer 03 — STOPPING
### Stopping Taste
*When to abandon, when to push, when done enough*

Graham's stopping rules are humble: most startups die from many small leaks rather than one fatal hit. The key signals are default-dead trajectory, the fatal pinch, broken founder energy, schlep avoidance, and hired-help culture replacing operator culture. Conversely, the signals to push harder are growth rate maintaining, ramen-profitability, and founder relentless resourcefulness intact. Trim losers slowly; let the outliers run.

---

**03.01 · Default Alive Is the Master Stop-Condition** `Essays`

**Rule:** If the company is default alive, push harder; if default dead, fix it within the next 90 days or wind down. Most companies don't realize they crossed into default-dead until 12 months too late.

**Test:** Calculate today: at current burn and growth rate, do you reach profitability before cash runs out? If no, the next 90 days are the only window that matters.

*Source: PG Essay: Default Alive or Default Dead (Oct 2015)*

---

**03.02 · The Fatal Pinch** `Essays`

**Rule:** When growth has stalled and runway is shrinking, the founder's first instinct (raise more money) is the worst move. The right move is cuts and refocus on growth — the round at default-dead pricing destroys the cap table.

**Test:** Have you been default-dead for >3 months without a clear product change to address it? If yes, raising will set you up for a death-spiral round; cut first.

*Source: PG Essay: The Fatal Pinch (Dec 2014)*

---

**03.03 · How to Die — The Antithesis Watchlist** `Essays`

**Rule:** The known startup-death modes — slow growth, founder despair, distraction, internal politics, board sclerosis — are predictable. Watch for them, name them when they appear.

**Test:** Quarterly, name which of the known death modes the company is closest to. If the founders cannot name one, they are not yet thinking honestly.

*Source: PG Essay: How Not to Die (Aug 2007); YC playbook*

---

**03.04 · Ramen Profitable as Stop-Loss** `Essays`

**Rule:** A startup that becomes ramen-profitable has bought the option to stop fundraising. Ramen-profit changes the negotiating posture with investors and gives the company an essentially infinite runway.

**Test:** Is there a path to ramen-profit within 6 months by cutting non-essential burn? If yes, the option is real; if no, the company is still capital-dependent.

*Source: PG Essay: Ramen Profitable (Jul 2009)*

---

**03.05 · Sustained Growth Below 1% w/w = Pivot or Wind Down** `Essays`

**Rule:** If weekly growth has been sub-1% for 12+ weeks despite real effort, the wave isn't there. Pivot the product (same problem, new artifact) or wind down — don't push harder on the existing version.

**Test:** 12-week trailing weekly growth rate. Below 1%? Pivot decision happens this month, not next quarter.

*Source: PG Essay: Startup = Growth (Sep 2012)*

---

**03.06 · The Subject: Airbnb Persistence Bound** `Essays`

**Rule:** Some companies that look unsalvageable are not — Airbnb selling cereal boxes to make payroll is the canonical example. Founders should be willing to do absurd things to keep the company alive when the underlying user love signal is intact.

**Test:** Do existing users genuinely love the product (cohort retention strong, NPS high, organic referral non-zero)? If yes, persist absurdly; if not, the cereal-box stunt won't save you.

*Source: PG Essay: Subject: Airbnb (Mar 2011)*

---

**03.07 · Top of My Todo List** `Essays`

**Rule:** On the deathbed, what would you regret? Apply the regret-minimization frame quarterly. Most founders' end-of-life regrets are about not pushing harder on the actual ambitious thing — not about pushing too hard.

**Test:** If the company died tomorrow, would the founders feel they had genuinely tried? If 'no, we held back,' the question is what they're afraid to do.

*Source: PG Essay: The Top of My Todo List (Apr 2012)*

---

**03.08 · Life Is Short** `Essays`

**Rule:** Life is short and time-allocation matters. Founders running half-hearted companies are paying the opportunity cost of the company they could have run instead. Wind down half-hearted bets fast.

**Test:** Are the founders' best years currently being spent on this? If they would honestly rather be doing something else, neither they nor their investors win by stretching it.

*Source: PG Essay: Life Is Short (Jan 2016)*

---

**03.09 · Power Law of YC Outcomes — Don't Trim Outliers** `Essays`

**Rule:** 1-2 companies per batch return more than the rest combined. Cutting losses on the boring ones is fine; cutting the weird-looking outlier early is the largest source of regret.

**Test:** Of the bottom 50% of the portfolio, what would change at the fund level if you killed them? If 'almost nothing,' don't waste energy. The energy should go to the top 10%.

*Source: PG Essay: Black Swan Farming (Sep 2012)*

---

**03.10 · What Happened to Yahoo — Culture Rot** `Essays`

**Rule:** Companies die organizationally before they die financially. Watch for the bureaucratic phase change: process for process's sake, internal politics consuming founder time, makers leaving, managers staying.

**Test:** What % of the founder's day is spent on internal vs external problems? Above 60% internal, the rot has started.

*Source: PG Essay: What Happened to Yahoo (Aug 2010)*

---

**03.11 · CEO Replacement Is a Stagnation Signal** `Essays`

**Rule:** Removing a founder for 'professional management' is, with very rare exceptions, a leading indicator of decline. Most professional CEOs maintain rather than build.

**Test:** Is the board debating bringing in 'adult supervision'? If yes, the conversation itself is the warning — investigate whether the founder is actually broken or whether the board is just impatient.

*Source: PG Essay: What Happened to Yahoo; Founder Mode (Sep 2024)*

---

**03.12 · The Trough of Sorrow** `Essays`

**Rule:** Between the launch bump and PMF, every startup goes through the trough — usage flatlines, the press loses interest, the team gets bored. Founders who don't recognize the trough as a phase, not a verdict, often quit at the bottom.

**Test:** Is the company in a flat post-launch period, or in a structural decline? Flat with strong cohort retention is normal trough; declining cohorts is real decline.

*Source: PG Essay: How Not to Die (Aug 2007); YC partner letters*

---

**03.13 · Founder Burnout Is the Real Cap** `Essays`

**Rule:** Long-arc companies survive on founder energy more than founder strategy. Burnout — physical or psychological — caps the company's altitude.

**Test:** Is the founder still relentlessly resourceful, or are they becoming reactive and defensive? The shift from initiative to reactivity is the burnout leading indicator.

*Source: PG Essay: Relentlessly Resourceful (Mar 2009); Founder Mode 2024*

---

**03.14 · Disconnecting Distraction Is Maintenance** `Essays`

**Rule:** If founders cannot disconnect, the depth of thought required for the next product cycle stops. The company will then drift on operational momentum until the wave ends.

**Test:** Has the founder had a deep-work block (4+ hours, no notifications) in the last 7 days? If never, drift is happening, even if the calendar looks productive.

*Source: PG Essay: Disconnecting Distraction (May 2008)*

---

**03.15 · Mean People Fail — Eject Toxic Hires** `Essays`

**Rule:** A toxic hire compounds. The cost of carrying a known-toxic person is not the seat — it's the cultural drift, the good-people-leaving second-order effect, and the founder's loss of moral authority.

**Test:** Has anyone in the team been known toxic for >30 days without action? That delay is the diagnostic; the founder is afraid of confrontation, and the team is reading that fear.

*Source: PG Essay: Mean People Fail (Nov 2014)*

---

**03.16 · Don't Let the Schleps Disappear** `Essays`

**Rule:** When the company gets large enough to delegate the unglamorous schleps, founder ground truth decays. The schleps are where the user's reality lives — protect direct exposure.

**Test:** Has the founder personally answered support tickets, walked a buying flow, or sat with a user this month? If never, ground truth is now arriving second-hand.

*Source: PG Essay: Schlep Blindness (Jan 2012); Founder Mode 2024*

---

**03.17 · Investor Pressure Is Usually Wrong** `Essays`

**Rule:** Investor pressure to grow faster, hire more, raise larger rounds is statistically wrong more often than right at the early stage. The founder's job is to run a real company; investor management is downstream.

**Test:** Does the most recent 'do this' from investors actually match what the user-pull data is telling you? If no, push back with data; if yes, it's not really investor pressure, it's market signal arriving via the board.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Convince Investors; The 18 Mistakes #14*

---

**03.18 · Don't Sell Too Early** `YC`

**Rule:** Most acquisition offers undervalue the long-arc. Decline strategics in the early years; consider only when the multiple is genuinely generational AND the founders' own appetite has waned.

**Test:** Is the offer at >10x your last round AND would the founders feel relief, not regret? Both required for a clean sale; only one = decline.

*Source: Startup School / YC playbook, 2010-2014*

---

**03.19 · Don't Hold Forever Either** `Essays`

**Rule:** The mirror failure mode: holding too long when the wave is over and the team has lost the relentlessly-resourceful energy. Wind down honestly rather than zombie-running.

**Test:** Is the founder still asking 'how do we win' or 'how do we keep going'? The shift to keep-going is the subtle marker of acceptance-of-decline.

*Source: PG Essay: How Not to Die; What Happened to Yahoo*

---

**03.20 · Refuse the Slow-Growth Acceptance** `Essays`

**Rule:** When weekly growth slows and the team rationalizes 'we're maturing,' it is more often that the wave passed. Distinguish maturation (cohort behavior stable, retention high) from stall (cohorts deteriorating).

**Test:** Are new-cohort retention curves the same as 12 months ago, better, or worse? Worse = stall masquerading as maturity.

*Source: PG Essay: Startup = Growth (Sep 2012)*

---

**03.21 · Trim Boring Losers, Keep Weird Outliers** `Essays`

**Rule:** The standard cut-the-losers heuristic is correct for boring companies but wrong for weird ones. Weird companies often look like losers in months 6-18 and breakout afterward.

**Test:** Is the underperformance coming from weak fundamentals or from a slow-burn weird thesis? Slow-burn weird thesis with active founder progress = hold; weak fundamentals = trim.

*Source: PG Essay: Black Swan Farming (Sep 2012)*

---

**03.22 · Eject Co-Founders Who Cannot Disagree-Then-Commit** `Essays`

**Rule:** A co-founder who keeps relitigating settled decisions is a slow killer of velocity. If after good-faith argument they cannot commit, the partnership is structurally broken.

**Test:** Have the same 2-3 disagreements re-emerged in board meetings/standups for >2 months? If yes, the founder split is in the future — make it sooner rather than later.

*Source: PG Essay: The 18 Mistakes #17; YC partner letters*

---

**03.23 · Don't Grow Headcount Past Communication Limit** `Essays`

**Rule:** There is a headcount above which the team can no longer communicate without process; beyond it, the founder's edge dissolves. Postpone hiring through the no-process zone as long as possible.

**Test:** Is everyone in the company still in 2-3 hops of communication of the founders? Once it's >3 hops for any team member, you've crossed into the process-dependent zone.

*Source: PG Essay: Founder Mode (Sep 2024); Hiring is Obsolete (May 2005)*

---

**03.24 · Don't Optimize the Pitch Past Reality** `Essays`

**Rule:** If the pitch is much better than the company, you'll close investors who later become disappointed. Calibrate the pitch to a flattering-but-accurate version of the truth.

**Test:** Are investors at 6-month check-ins reacting positively or with surprise? Surprise = the pitch was ahead of reality; investors will become reluctant follow-ons.

*Source: PG Essay: How to Convince Investors (Aug 2013)*

---

**03.25 · When the Wave Ends, Wind Down Fast** `Essays`

**Rule:** When the underlying technology wave has ended (mobile saturation, web2 social maxed out), the right move is fast wind-down or sharp pivot — not a slow grind on a fading wave.

**Test:** Has the wave's adoption curve flattened or declined for >12 months? If yes, the company is in salvage mode; the question is exit speed.

*Source: PG Essay: Microsoft Is Dead (Apr 2007); Black Swan Farming*

---

**03.26 · Stop the Process Creep Early** `Essays`

**Rule:** The drift from operator culture to manager culture happens gradually — first one extra meeting, then one new VP, then one approval workflow. By the time it's visible, it's expensive to undo.

**Test:** How many recurring meetings exist that did not exist 12 months ago? Above ~3 net additions, you've started the manager-mode drift; founder-mode requires conscious resistance.

*Source: PG Essay: Founder Mode (Sep 2024); Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule*

## 2026 Overlay

### Where the Alpha Is
- AI-as-tools that genuinely change knowledge workers' weekly output (vertical AI, agent platforms with real distribution)
- Schlep-heavy AI applications — legal, healthcare, regulated, B2B back-office — where the schlep is the moat
- Founder-mode operating playbooks at 100-1000 person companies — coaching, peer networks, founder retention through scale
- Hardware-meets-software in non-glamorous categories (manufacturing, robotics, infrastructure) where prototyping costs collapsed
- Niche professional tools where 5-7% w/w organic growth is achievable on small named-user wedges
- Ramen-profitable indie SaaS that can compound without VC capital

### Auto-Reject
- Pure LLM wrappers without distribution moat or schlep barrier
- Solo founders for venture-shaped outcomes
- Pre-PMF teams >24 months on the same plan with sub-1% w/w growth
- Default-dead companies in the fatal pinch raising at flat valuations
- Companies that have crossed into manager-mode drift (recurring meetings replacing maker output)
- Founders mid-burnout still raising on past momentum
- Derivative ideas (Foo-for-X clones)
- Companies whose model requires dark patterns or coercive defaults

# The Hamming Doctrine — Three Evaluation Layers (Deep Dive)

> *"What are the important problems of your field? Why aren't you working on them?"*

A three-layer filter for choosing and executing research and technical work. Distilled from 'You and Your Research' (Bell Labs lecture, 1986), 'The Art of Doing Science and Engineering: Learning to Learn' (NPS lectures, 1995-1998), Hamming's Turing Award lecture (1968), Bell Labs operating history (1945-1986), and the error-correcting codes paper (1950) as a case study in the doctrine itself.

**77 doctrines across 3 layers.** Every doctrine carries a *rule*, a concrete *test*, and its *source*.

---

## Layer 1 — Problem-Selection Taste *(31 doctrines)*
*What is worth working on at all.*

Hamming's central observation: most scientists and engineers spend their careers on safe, publishable, forgettable problems. The discipline of problem selection — asking not 'what can I solve?' but 'what would matter?' — separates great scientists from good ones. This layer encodes Hamming's criteria for identifying problems worth a career.

### From *You and Your Research* (Bell Labs, 1986)

- **The Monday Morning Question** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Every Monday morning ask: 'What are the important problems of my field?' Then ask: 'Why am I not working on them?' Most people have never seriously posed either.
- **Importance Before Tractability** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science, Ch. 1)* — Start with importance — what would matter? — then find tractability. Working on an unimportant problem perfectly is wasted work.
- **The Great Scientist vs. Good Scientist Test** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — The difference is not intelligence but courage: to work on important problems, to change fields when necessary, to think long-term about what matters.
- **Courage Is the Prerequisite** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — It takes courage to work on important problems because they have a real chance of failure. Courage, not genius, separates great scientists.
- **The Reasonable Chance Criterion** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Important problems must have a non-trivial probability of being solvable with available tools. Both importance AND tractability are required.
- **Open Door vs. Closed Door Problems** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Slightly open doors are the richest territory. The crack in the door is the secret: a technique, dataset, or framing that makes it tractable for you but not for others.
- **Plant Seeds Broadly, Tend the Ones That Grow** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science)* — Maintain a portfolio of 5-10 problems in the back of your mind. When one becomes tractable, you'll be positioned to move fast.
- **The 10% Rule** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Invest roughly 10% of working time in long-shot, potentially transformative problems even with no clear path forward.
- **Reject Safe Mediocrity** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Choosing problems to guarantee a result optimizes for career safety at the cost of career significance. The choice to be safe is a choice to be forgettable.
- **Think About the Next Problem During This One** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Great scientists discover the next problem while working on the current one. Maintain a parallel thread: what does this result generalize to?
- **The Long-View Selection Frame** *(Art of Doing Science, Ch. 1)* — Ask: will this work matter in 20 years? Not 'will it be cited' — will it have changed something about how the world works?
- **Work Where You Have an Unfair Advantage** *(You and Your Research; Bell Labs history)* — The best problem is one where your specific background, access, or intuition gives you an edge others don't have.
- **Avoid the Fashionable Problem Trap** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Fashionable problems attract the most talent and are therefore the most competitive. The best problems are neglected: important, solvable, but not yet fashionable.
- **Ask Your Field's Elders the Hard Question** *(You and Your Research; NPS lectures)* — Ask senior researchers: 'What are the most important problems nobody is working on?' Their answers often surprise.
- **Convert Failure Into a Problem Definition** *(Bell Labs technical notes; Art of Doing Science)* — When an approach fails, convert the failure into a better problem definition. Every failed attack narrows the solution space.
- **The Boredom Filter** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — If a problem doesn't obsess you — if you're not thinking about it in the shower — it's probably not important enough to justify years of your life.
- **The Impact Multiplier Filter** *(Art of Doing Science; Hamming codes paper, 1950)* — Prefer problems whose solution becomes infrastructure that others build on. The highest-impact problems are those whose solutions enable many other solutions.
- **The Rephrasing Trick** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science, Ch. 3)* — When a problem seems unsolvable, rephrase it. Hamming repeatedly solved problems by asking 'what is the real question here?'
- **The Nobel Prize Test** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Ask: if this work succeeds, could a reasonable Nobel committee consider it? The question forces honest evaluation of whether the work is genuinely foundational.
- **Narrow Deep vs. Wide Shallow** *(Art of Doing Science; You and Your Research)* — The signature of great scientists is one or two deep results requiring years of focused effort, not a broad portfolio of minor contributions.
- **Problems With High Leverage** *(Hamming codes paper; Art of Doing Science)* — Seek problems whose solution removes a fundamental constraint, eliminating entire categories of downstream problems.
- **The Contrarian Problem List** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — The most important problems are often considered intractable by the mainstream. Maintain a list of problems experts call hopeless and evaluate each for hidden tractability.
- **Results Must Be Communicable** *(Art of Doing Science, Ch. 4)* — A result that cannot be communicated has no impact. Choose problems where the solution, if found, can be clearly explained and verified.
- **The Simplicity Standard** *(Art of Doing Science; You and Your Research)* — The most important results are almost always expressible simply. If your result requires extreme complexity to state, you may not have found the right formulation.
- **Avoid the Thesis-Continuation Trap** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Most researchers spend 10 years extending their PhD thesis. The thesis was a training problem; your career requires finding the real problem.
- **Read the History of Your Field** *(Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures)* — Understanding how great problems were identified and solved tells you where the next great problems are likely to come from.
- **The Tool-Problem Mismatch** *(You and Your Research; Bell Labs technical culture)* — Often the reason an important problem remains unsolved is that the available tools are wrong for it. Making the tool may be the real work.
- **The Aesthetic Criterion** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science)* — Great problems are often beautiful in their formulation. Train the aesthetic sense — ugly, complicated problem statements rarely lead to foundational results.
- **Follow the Anomalies** *(Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures)* — Anomalies — places where the current theory doesn't fit the data — are the most reliable indicator of where the next important breakthrough will come from.
- **Don't Let Fashion Define Importance** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — What gets funded and published is often determined by fashion, not importance. Judge importance by consequences, not by the field's current attention.
- **Field-Selection Is Career-Defining** *(Art of Doing Science, Ch. 2)* — Your field determines the ceiling of your impact. Periodically re-evaluate whether your field has a future and whether its important problems are still unsolved.

## Layer 2 — Approach Taste *(29 doctrines)*
*Given the problem, which angle to attack from.*

Hamming was obsessed with how great scientists work — not just what they worked on, but how they operated day-to-day. This layer distils his observations about style, method, and the habits that separate productive researchers from unproductive ones. The doctrine is particularly strong on communication, emotional investment, and the underrated role of open collaboration.

### Working style and method

- **Work With Your Door Open** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Scientists who close their door get more done in the short term and less in the long term. The interruptions provide cross-pollination that produces breakthroughs.
- **Emotional Investment Is Not Optional** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — You cannot do great work without being emotionally invested. Scientists who treat their work as 9-to-5 produce 9-to-5 results.
- **Subconscious Working** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Deliberately load a hard problem into your mind before sleeping or before routine tasks. Many breakthroughs come during commutes, walks, or the first minutes after waking.
- **The Style of the Great Scientist** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science)* — Every great scientist has a recognizable style. Developing your own is not vanity; it's the accumulation of specific intuitions, tools, and aesthetic sensibility.
- **Tolerate Ambiguity** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Great scientists are comfortable sitting with ambiguity for months or years while clarity slowly emerges. Most researchers avoid this discomfort.
- **Write It Up, Even Early** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science, Ch. 4)* — Writing forces a clarity of thought that thinking alone never achieves. Write current understanding even before results — the act reveals gaps.
- **Give Talks Before You're Ready** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — The discipline of presenting half-formed ideas accelerates research. Questions from an audience reveal blind spots that don't emerge in solo work.
- **Learn the Style of Communication in Your Field** *(Art of Doing Science, Ch. 4)* — Brilliance that isn't communicated effectively is wasted. Bad writing and bad presentations have buried good science.
- **Prepare for Luck** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Pasteur's maxim: chance favors the prepared mind. Luck plays a large role in great discoveries, but only prepared minds can convert it into breakthroughs.
- **Systems Over One-Off Solutions** *(Hamming codes paper; Digital Filters, 1977)* — When attacking a problem, ask not just 'how do I solve this case?' but 'what general method would solve all cases of this type?'
- **Use the Best Tools Available** *(Art of Doing Science; Bell Labs computing history)* — Hamming was an early computer adopter precisely because better tools enable better research. Don't be sentimental about old methods.
- **Master the Mathematics of Your Field** *(Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures)* — Whatever your field, mathematics is its deepest language. Researchers who avoid the mathematics limit themselves to surface-level understanding.
- **The Confidence to Be Wrong** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — The fear of being wrong keeps researchers in safe territory. Great scientists make bold claims, are wrong publicly, and revise. Being cautious is not the same as being rigorous.
- **Read Broadly, Think Narrowly** *(Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures)* — The best breakthroughs often come from applying tools or insights from one field to problems in another. Broad reading requires deliberate effort.
- **The Three Components of Great Work** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Three essentials: a significant problem, sustained attack over a long period, courage to push to a fundamental result rather than stopping at a preliminary finding.
- **Think Consistently About the Future** *(Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures)* — Devote some part of every week to where your field is going in 10-20 years. Getting ahead of the wave is a major source of competitive advantage.
- **Collaboration as Signal-Amplifier** *(You and Your Research; Bell Labs culture)* — Working with smart people who know different things amplifies your signal. Hamming used Bell Labs' concentration of talent deliberately.
- **The Long Fuse** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — The most important work often takes years or decades to be recognized. Calibrate on long-term importance, not immediate recognition.
- **The Friday Afternoon Experiment** *(You and Your Research; Bell Labs research culture)* — Set aside time each week for undirected exploration. Some fraction of the most important Bell Labs discoveries came from undirected play.
- **The Error-Correcting Mind** *(Hamming codes paper; Art of Doing Science)* — Build redundancy into your thinking. Reach the same conclusion from multiple different angles. A single-path insight may be an artifact of that path.
- **Study Your Own Thinking** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science)* — Meta-cognition is underrated as a research skill. Understand your own cognitive patterns: when most productive, what conditions produce best insights.
- **Keep a Notebook of Great Ideas** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Maintain a running list of ideas, including half-formed ones. Revisit monthly. Many great results start as a vague sense that 'something is possible here.'
- **Attack From Multiple Angles Simultaneously** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Launch several attack angles simultaneously. Each failed attack reveals structure about the problem. The combination often suggests the right approach.
- **The Articulation Test** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science)* — If you can't articulate why a problem is important to a smart non-expert in five minutes, you don't understand it deeply enough.
- **Maintain the Beginner's Eye** *(Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures)* — Experts miss solutions obvious to beginners because they carry the baggage of knowing what 'should' work. Challenge unstated assumptions periodically.
- **The Work Compounds** *(You and Your Research; Art of Doing Science)* — Each year of work makes the next more productive. This compounding requires working on related problems over a long period — too many pivots prevent it.
- **Solve the Meta-Problem** *(Art of Doing Science; Turing Award lecture, 1968)* — Sometimes the most important work is building infrastructure — methods, languages, tools — that allows many people to solve object-level problems.
- **Manage Your Ambition Carefully** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Too little ambition produces safe work. Too much produces paralysis. Right calibration: the most important problem where you have a plausible path in 3-5 years.
- **Persistence on the Right Problem** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — There is a crucial difference between persistence (continuing to attack an important problem) and stubbornness (continuing a failed approach). Persist on the problem; abandon the approach readily.

## Layer 3 — Stopping Taste *(17 doctrines)*
*When to abandon, when to push, when done enough.*

Hamming had precise views on when to stop. The core insight: stopping is a problem-selection decision in reverse. The same criteria that determine whether to start a problem also determine whether to continue it.

### Stopping signals from the research record

- **The 'So What' Test** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Before writing the paper, ask 'so what?' What does this result enable? What does it generalize to? If answers are 'not much,' the result may need extension.
- **Stop When the Field Has Passed You** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Signs: defending your results against the new generation rather than building on them; methods being taught in textbooks. Time to change direction.
- **Know When to Change Fields** *(You and Your Research; Hamming's career history)* — Hamming changed fields at least three times. Each change was driven by the same logic: important problems here are solved or blocked; that field's are accessible to me.
- **Stop When You've Solved the General Problem** *(Hamming codes paper, 1950)* — Know when a result is 'done' — the general principle is established and the field can take it from here. Diminishing returns on refinement are real.
- **The Failed-Three-Attacks Rule** *(You and Your Research; Hamming's problem-switching pattern)* — Three fundamentally different attacks all failed → stop temporarily. Set it aside for 3-6 months. The subconscious needs distance.
- **The Diminishing-Returns Threshold** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Distinguish diminishing returns on the current approach (switch approaches) from diminishing returns on the problem itself (switch problems).
- **Abandon Preliminaries That Have Become Ends** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — When the stepping stone has become the destination without reaching the destination, the project may have lost its purpose.
- **The Recognition Trap** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Fame changes what researchers work on, almost always for the worse. The temptation is to defend reputation by working on safe problems. Watch for this drift.
- **The Management Transition** *(You and Your Research; Bell Labs promotion culture)* — Management should be a deliberate choice, not a default promotion. Taking the management path should be consciously aligned with your goals.
- **The Last Paper Test** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — Ask: if I wrote one more paper and then stopped, what should it be? If you're not actively working on that paper, ask why.
- **When a Field Is Mined Out** *(You and Your Research; Hamming's migration to computer science)* — The best people are leaving for adjacent fields. When the best people leave, follow them. Their revealed preferences are data about where the frontier has moved.
- **The Age-and-Stage Calibration** *(Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures)* — Early career: develop taste and tools. Mid-career: do the most important work you're capable of. Late career: teach, communicate, and identify the next generation's important problems.
- **Stop Optimizing for Output, Start Optimizing for Legacy** *(Art of Doing Science; NPS lectures)* — At some point the right metric shifts from 'how many papers this year' to 'what will I leave behind that matters.'
- **When to Publish vs. When to Continue** *(Art of Doing Science; Bell Labs publication culture)* — Publish when: central claim is established, checked via two independent routes, most important implications are worked out. If any one is missing, continue.
- **Don't Over-Defend** *(You and Your Research, 1986)* — A correct result will ultimately be recognized. Over-defending is a distraction. Write one rebuttal if necessary, then prove the critics wrong with better work.
- **The Generalization Imperative Before Stopping** *(Hamming codes paper; Art of Doing Science)* — Before stopping on a result, ask: what is the most general form of this? Always attempt the generalization before declaring the work done.
- **Exit Status Fields** *(You and Your Research; NPS lectures)* — When a field rewards prestige proxies over important results, the best work moves elsewhere. Know the ecology of your field and migrate accordingly.

## 2026 Overlay

### Where the alpha is
- AI-assisted scientific discovery: problems that were computationally intractable are now tractable — high-value targets
- Biology and materials science: the 'atoms' equivalent of the software revolution, now accessible
- Fundamental mathematics with real-world consequences: the Hamming-Shannon legacy suggests underinvestment here
- Tools for science: better research infrastructure compounds for the entire field
- Cross-disciplinary problems at the intersection of ML and physical science

### Auto-reject
- Research that optimizes for publication count over importance
- Fields where the primary innovation is in applying existing ML methods rather than in the science itself
- Problems that require regulatory capture or political consensus to matter
- Work that is important only within a narrow sub-field with no generalization potential
- Research programs driven by funding availability rather than problem importance

## Verdict logic

Run each doctrine against the candidate research project or technical agenda. Mark **Pass / Weak / Fail** with notes. Then:

- **Kill** — any single Fail in Layer 1 (Problem-Selection), or 3+ Fails total
- **Proceed** — zero Fails, ≤2 Weaks, ≥60% Pass coverage across scored doctrines
- **Reconsider** — anything else

The Monday Morning Question is the master filter: if the work doesn't survive 'what are the important problems of my field and why am I working on this one?', all subsequent doctrine evaluation is answering the wrong question.

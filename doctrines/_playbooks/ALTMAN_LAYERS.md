# The Altman Doctrine — Three Evaluation Layers (Deep Dive)

> *"The most important things are to work on the right problem, find the right team, and don't run out of money."*

A three-layer filter for building transformative companies. Distilled from 'How to be Successful' (samaltman.com, 2019), the 'Startup Playbook' (YC), 'What I Wish Someone Had Told Me' (2023), 'The Intelligence Age' (2024), the Stanford CS183B lectures (2014), Lex Fridman #367 and #419, the Acquired podcast interview (2023), and the full OpenAI blog corpus (2019-2026).

**64 doctrines across 3 layers.** Every doctrine carries a *rule*, a concrete *test*, and its *source*.

---

## Layer 1 — Problem-Selection Taste *(20 doctrines)*
*What is worth working on at all.*

Altman's problem-selection doctrine is organized around one principle: civilizational stakes. Problems worth working on should matter at the scale of humanity, not just a market segment. The filter combines importance with timing — the right problem at the wrong moment is as bad as the wrong problem. The biggest breakthroughs often seem unambitious at the outset, but their potential is visible to those with the right frame.

### From the Startup Playbook and 'How to be Successful'

- **Work on What Matters at Civilization Scale** *(How to be Successful, 2019; Lex Fridman #367)* — The most important work addresses problems that, if solved, materially improve the condition of humanity. Every founder should answer: 'If this succeeds, what changes about the world?'
- **The Market Timing Imperative** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B, 2014)* — Most failed startups had the right idea at the wrong time. The best startups ride a wave that is just beginning — where the enabling conditions have just arrived.
- **Founder-Market Fit Over Product-Market Fit** *(How to be Successful; Stanford CS183B)* — Product-market fit can be found by many founders; founder-market fit determines whether you can outlast the competition to find it.
- **Hard Problems Attract Better People** *(How to be Successful; Startup Playbook)* — Ambitious, hard problems attract the best talent. If the mission is too modest, you'll struggle to hire the extraordinary people you need.
- **The Adjacency to Transformative Technology** *(The Intelligence Age, 2024; OpenAI blog)* — The most powerful startups are often those building applications that only become possible once a transformative technology arrives. Identify what's just arrived.
- **Non-Consensus and Right** *(Startup Playbook; Acquired podcast, 2023)* — Consensus opportunities are oversubscribed by definition. The only opportunities with exceptional upside are those where you believe something true that most smart people don't yet believe.
- **Favor Markets That Will Grow** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Assess the market you want to exist in 10 years, not the market that exists today. The correct TAM analysis is forward-looking.
- **The Problem Should Be a Hair-on-Fire Problem** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — The best startup problems are ones where users are actively suffering. Aspirins sell themselves; vitamins require convincing.
- **Beware the Idea You Can't Give Away** *(Startup Playbook)* — If 100 smart people find the idea interesting but nobody urgently wants it, you have an interesting idea, not a business. The test is behavior change, not approval.
- **The Compounding Startup** *(Startup Playbook; How to be Successful)* — Startup ideas that compound — where success at step 1 creates the platform for step 2 — produce disproportionate outcomes. Name the first three stages explicitly.
- **The AGI Frame for Problem Selection** *(The Intelligence Age, 2024; OpenAI blog)* — AI will compress the timeline on nearly every scientific and technological problem. Every founder should ask: which problems does AI unlock in my domain?
- **Neglected and Important** *(How to be Successful; Startup Playbook)* — The best problems are both important and neglected. Important without neglect = a hundred competitors. Neglected without importance = no market.
- **Startup Problems Should Feel Slightly Crazy** *(Startup Playbook; Acquired, 2023)* — If your idea sounds completely reasonable to everyone, it probably isn't ambitious enough to be a great startup.
- **Early Revenue Is a Filter, Not a Goal** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Finding users who will pay immediately is a signal that you've found a real problem. Revenue at the earliest stage is primarily feedback.
- **Starting Too Small Is Usually Right** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Starting narrow and dominating before expanding is almost always better than addressing a broad market from day one.
- **The Problem That Will Exist at Scale** *(Startup Playbook)* — Some problems only become visible at scale. The best companies anticipate these problems and build for the scaled world before it arrives.
- **Work on Problems Where You Have Unfair Insight** *(How to be Successful; Startup Playbook)* — The best startup ideas come from direct experience with the problem. Unfair insight is the most sustainable competitive advantage.
- **The 10-Year Test** *(How to be Successful)* — Would this problem still be worth solving in 10 years even if technology changed dramatically? If yes, you're working on an enduring human need.
- **Avoid Solutions Looking for Problems** *(Startup Playbook)* — Start with the problem, not the technology. If your pitch starts with 'we built X, now looking for applications,' the approach is backward.
- **The Defensibility Horizon** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Every startup needs a specific answer to 'why won't a big company copy this in 18 months?' — network effects, switching costs, proprietary tech, regulatory moat, or an unreplicable team.

## Layer 2 — Approach Taste *(29 doctrines)*
*Given the problem, which angle to attack from.*

Altman's approach doctrine is organized around speed, conviction, and talent density. The core thesis: the biggest advantage a startup has over an incumbent is speed — the ability to make and execute decisions in days that would take a large company months. Every approach doctrine is in service of maintaining that speed without sacrificing quality.

### Speed and execution

- **Speed Is a Moat** *(What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook)* — Every week of faster iteration compounds. Speed is not just an operating preference — it is a competitive strategy. The team that ships faster learns faster.
- **Make Something People Want, Above All Else** *(Startup Playbook; YC core doctrine)* — The primary job of every startup is to make something people actually use, recommend, and would miss if it disappeared.
- **Growth Is the Primary Feedback Loop** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Consistent week-over-week growth is the single most important signal. 5-7% WoW sustained for a year indicates product-market fit has been found.
- **Do the Unscalable Thing First** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — In the early stage, do things that don't scale: personally recruit every user, manually perform operations that will later be automated.
- **Talk to Users Constantly** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Direct, unmediated founder conversation with users is the highest-value activity for most founders in the early stage. Not surveys — founders talking directly to users.
- **User Obsession Over Competitor Obsession** *(Startup Playbook)* — Competitor obsession leads to feature parity races. User obsession leads to genuine breakthroughs. If your roadmap is driven by competitor analysis, you're reacting.

### Team and talent

- **Hire People Better Than You at Every Role** *(How to be Successful; What I Wish, 2023)* — Exceptional people are not incrementally better than good people — they are 10x better. They elevate everyone around them.
- **Recruit Missionaries, Not Mercenaries** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Missionaries optimize for the mission. They are more resilient, more creative in constrained environments, more trustworthy with hard problems.
- **Fire Fast, Carefully** *(What I Wish, 2023)* — Keeping underperformers too long is one of the most common and costly mistakes. The right time to fire is usually 3-6 months earlier than most founders actually do it.
- **Give Equity to the People Who Deserve It** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Equity is the primary alignment mechanism. People who hold meaningful equity think like owners; those who hold little think like employees.
- **The Right Investors Change the Trajectory** *(Startup Playbook)* — The best investors bring more than capital — access to talent, honest advice, pattern recognition. The wrong investors bring money and noise.

### Mission and conviction

- **Clear Communication of Mission and Metrics** *(What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook)* — Every person should state the mission in one sentence and the three most important metrics the company is tracking this quarter.
- **The Self-Belief Prerequisite** *(How to be Successful; What I Wish, 2023)* — Founders who need external validation to maintain conviction will fold when things get hard. Conviction must be internally sourced.
- **Move Fast and Have High Standards Simultaneously** *(What I Wish, 2023)* — Speed and quality are not necessarily in conflict. The best teams ship fast AND maintain high standards — because they've internalized what matters.
- **The Priority Stack** *(What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook)* — Every week, the company should have one priority above all others. Companies that have 10 priorities have zero priorities.
- **Long-Term Thinking in Short-Term Actions** *(How to be Successful; various Altman interviews)* — Think in decades; act in weeks. Every short-term action should be the week's best expression of a long-term strategy.

### Operational fundamentals

- **Fundraise Efficiently, Then Get Back to Work** *(Startup Playbook; What I Wish, 2023)* — Fundraising is a necessary interruption, not the job. The best founders raise from the first investors who offer strong terms, then return to product work.
- **The Board Is a Tool, Not a Boss** *(Startup Playbook; Acquired, 2023)* — The best founders respect their boards, keep them informed, and use them as sounding boards — but make decisions based on their own judgment and user data.
- **Manage Your Energy, Not Your Schedule** *(How to be Successful; What I Wish, 2023)* — The founder's most important resource is cognitive energy. Protect large blocks of time for the highest-leverage thinking.
- **Think in Exponentials, Not Linears** *(The Intelligence Age, 2024; Moore's Law for Everything, 2021)* — Most people think in linear extrapolation. Train yourself to ask: what does this look like if the growth rate holds for 10 years?
- **Own Your Mistakes** *(What I Wish, 2023)* — Teams that say 'we were wrong, here's what we're doing differently' recover faster and earn more trust than teams that explain why external factors were primarily responsible.
- **Management by Context, Not Control** *(What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook)* — Provide context — why decisions are being made, what the priorities are — rather than control. Context-rich teams make better decisions locally.
- **Network as Strategic Asset** *(How to be Successful; Stanford CS183B)* — The person you hire in 3 years should be someone you've been cultivating a relationship with for 2 years. Build networks before you need them.
- **Compound Personal Credibility** *(How to be Successful; What I Wish, 2023)* — Every accurate prediction, every honest acknowledgment of failure, every promise kept adds to the credibility stock. Manage it actively.
- **Frugality as a Feature** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — The discipline of operating frugally produces better decisions. Startups that raise too much early make expensive, reversible mistakes without the learning that scarcity forces.
- **The Weekly All-Hands as Truth Machine** *(What I Wish, 2023; OpenAI culture)* — Share both good and bad news transparently. Teams can handle the truth; they can't function well without it.
- **The AI Transformation of Software Development** *(The Intelligence Age, 2024)* — A 5-person team with the right AI tools can build what previously required 50. Ambition should scale up accordingly.

## Layer 3 — Stopping Taste *(15 doctrines)*
*When to abandon, when to push, when done enough.*

Altman's stopping doctrine distinguishes between stopping signals that come from outside (bad press, investor skepticism, competitive pressure) and stopping signals that come from inside (users don't actually want this, growth has stopped for unexplained reasons, the team has lost conviction). The former are often noise; the latter are almost always signal.

### Primary stopping signals

- **Growth Is the Primary Stay Signal** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — If a startup is growing consistently — 5-7%+ WoW in the early stage — almost every other problem is solvable. Lack of growth is the primary stop signal.
- **The 18-Month Signal** *(Startup Playbook)* — If a startup hasn't found meaningful traction in 18 months despite genuine attempts with different approaches, consider whether the problem or the founding team is the constraint.
- **Pivot the Product, Not the Mission** *(Startup Playbook)* — Founders who pivot the mission rarely recover. Pivoting the product — finding a different solution to the same problem — is healthy and often necessary.
- **Talent Density as the Stopping Test** *(What I Wish, 2023)* — Ask periodically: would you hire every person on your team today? If the 'no' list exceeds 20%, you have a significant talent density problem.
- **Kill Features Without Mercy** *(Startup Playbook)* — Features that don't serve the core value proposition dilute focus and slow the team. Kill them with the same energy you'd bring to a key hire decision.
- **Ignore Most Outside Noise** *(What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook)* — Press, Twitter, competitor announcements — almost none of this is useful signal. The signal that matters is user behavior.
- **When to Stop Fundraising** *(Startup Playbook)* — Stop as soon as you have enough runway to reach your next important milestone. More money than needed is a distraction.
- **The Mission Test for Staying** *(Startup Playbook)* — Founders should stay as long as they are the best people to execute the mission and remain genuinely committed to it. The right reason to leave is that someone else would execute better.
- **The Great vs. Good Company Threshold** *(How to be Successful; Stanford CS183B)* — At every stage: are we on the great path or the good path? If good, what would need to change to get to great?
- **Stop When the Founders Have Lost Conviction** *(Startup Playbook; Acquired, 2023)* — When founders have genuinely lost conviction in the mission, the company is effectively over regardless of runway or growth metrics.
- **The Acqui-hire vs. Real Exit Test** *(Startup Playbook)* — When considering an exit, ask honestly: is this a real acquisition or a team rescue? The latter is fine, but be honest about what value has actually been created.
- **When to Stop Scaling** *(Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B)* — Scaling before product-market fit is the most common cause of expensive startup failure. The signals: growth requires active selling, churn is high, users aren't referring others unprompted.
- **The 'Would You Start This Today?' Test** *(Various Altman interviews; Startup Playbook)* — Periodically ask: knowing everything you know now, would you start this company today? Apply this question every six months.
- **Stop Protecting Failing Bets** *(What I Wish, 2023; OpenAI operating history)* — The willingness to acknowledge a failing bet and reallocate is one of the most valuable executive skills. It requires public acknowledgment of error.
- **The Values Test for Staying** *(What I Wish, 2023)* — When continuing requires systematic dishonesty to investors, employees, or users about what is working and what isn't, stop. The damage to personal credibility outweighs the potential upside.

## 2026 Overlay

### Where the alpha is
- AI-native applications: products fundamentally restructured around AI capabilities, not just AI-augmented versions of old products
- Energy infrastructure: AI's energy demands make compute-adjacent energy companies among the most important businesses of the next decade
- Universal basic compute: access to AI tools as infrastructure — the policy and business implications
- Healthcare and drug discovery: AI-enabled acceleration of the longest-cycle industry now shows near-term results
- Physical AI: robotics, autonomous systems, and AI that operates in the physical world

### Auto-reject
- AI wrappers with no proprietary data, model, or workflow moat — will be commoditized
- Consumer social products without a clear network effect defense against platform algorithm changes
- B2B SaaS in categories where AI is replacing the workflow the software was designed to support
- Business models that depend on regulatory arbitrage as the primary moat
- Any startup that cannot answer 'why now?' with a specific technology or market change from the last 3 years

## Verdict logic

Run each doctrine against the candidate startup. Mark **Pass / Weak / Fail** with notes. Then:

- **Kill** — any single Fail in Layer 1 (Problem-Selection), or 3+ Fails total
- **Proceed** — zero Fails, ≤2 Weaks, ≥60% Pass coverage across scored doctrines
- **Reconsider** — anything else

The Growth signal is the master Layer 3 rule: consistent week-over-week growth at the appropriate rate indicates product-market fit has been found and most other problems are solvable. Without it, all other execution quality is addressing the wrong variable.

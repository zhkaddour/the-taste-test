# VOL. 18 / THIEL
## The Thiel Doctrine

> What important truth do very few people agree with you on?

**Subject:** Peter Thiel

A three-layer filter for evaluating startup ideas. Distilled from a deep read of Zero to One (chapter-by-chapter, page-cited), the long-form essays, the spoken corpus (Cowen, Rogan, Weinstein, Hoover, Bari Weiss, ARC, the Antichrist lectures), Founders Fund's revealed portfolio taste, and the operating record at PayPal/Palantir/Clarium with 2020-2026 updates. Every doctrine carries a rule, a concrete test, and its source.

---
## Problem-Selection Taste
*What is worth working on at all*

The filter is built to reject the consensus problem. Startups die not because they execute badly on a good problem but because the problem itself is mimetic, indeterminate, or already-priced. Pick problems that move 0 to 1, look too small to be a business, and contain a nameable secret.

### The Contrarian Question
**Rule:** What important truth do very few people agree with you on? What valuable company is nobody building?

**Test:** Founder must produce a one-sentence answer that visibly discomforts a credentialed listener. Consensus dressed as contrarianism ("AI will be huge") fails.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.1; Cowen, Stanford CS183

**Chapter:** Ch.1

### Vertical-vs-Horizontal Progress Filter
**Rule:** Reject ideas that merely take 1 to n (copying what works, scaling, globalizing). Pursue 0 to 1 (vertical/intensive progress, doing what nobody has done).

**Test:** Ask: is this a typewriter→100 typewriters move (horizontal) or typewriter→word processor (vertical)? If founder cannot articulate the 0-to-1 step, pass.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.1 (pp.7-8)

**Chapter:** Ch.1

### Globalization-Without-Technology Catastrophe
**Rule:** In a world of scarce resources, spreading old ways of creating wealth is unsustainable. A startup whose thesis is 'bring X to market Y' (pure globalization) is fragile.

**Test:** Does this startup require new tech, or just diffusion of existing tech to new geographies? Diffusion-only = pass.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.1 (p.10)

**Chapter:** Ch.1

### Largest-Convincible-Group Sizing
**Rule:** A startup is the largest group of people you can convince of a plan to build a different future — not the smallest viable team, not a movement.

**Test:** Can the founders articulate a plan radical enough that only a small group could believe it, yet concrete enough to convince that group? If consensus-acceptable, it's not a startup.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.1 (p.12)

**Chapter:** Ch.1

### Value-Created vs Value-Captured Wedge
**Rule:** Creating value is necessary but not sufficient — you must capture a meaningful share. Airlines create hundreds of billions and capture 37 cents per trip; Google captures 21%.

**Test:** Estimate total customer value created and the % the company can plausibly capture in steady state. If capture < ~10%, the business is structurally an airline, not a Google.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.3 (pp.24-25)

**Chapter:** Ch.3

### Anti-Commodity Filter
**Rule:** If you want to create and capture lasting value, don't build an undifferentiated commodity business. Capitalism and competition are opposites.

**Test:** Outsiders looking at the firm see it on a competition↔monopoly continuum near the middle; the reality is binary. Demand evidence the company is at the monopoly extreme.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.3 (pp.26-27)

**Chapter:** Ch.3

### Margin Test for Monopoly
**Rule:** Margins reveal market structure. Airlines: 0.2%. Google: 21%. The order-of-magnitude gap, not branding, identifies real monopoly.

**Test:** Compare projected steady-state operating margin to comparable competitive industries. Within 2x of a known commodity market = treat as competitive regardless of narrative.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.3 (p.24)

**Chapter:** Ch.3

### Static-vs-Dynamic Monopoly Distinction
**Rule:** Monopolies are bad in a static world (rent collection) but good in a dynamic world — creative monopolies add new categories of abundance and finance long-term R&D.

**Test:** Does this monopoly arise from inventing new abundance (Apple/iPhone, Google/search) or from rent-extraction (license capture, regulatory moat alone)? Only fund the former.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.3 (p.34)

**Chapter:** Ch.3

### The Secret
**Rule:** A monopoly is downstream of a secret. Two kinds — about nature, or about people.

**Test:** Why has no one tried this? must have a non-obvious answer. No nameable secret = incremental.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.8

**Chapter:** Ch.8

### Two Kinds of Secrets — Nature vs People
**Rule:** Ask both: what secrets is nature not telling you? AND what secrets are people not telling you? Secrets about people are underappreciated because no PhD is required — just the question 'what are people not allowed to talk about?'

**Test:** For our thesis, what is the nature-secret AND what is the people-secret? Strongest theses combine both.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.8 (pp.97-98)

**Chapter:** Ch.8

### Look Where No One Else Is Looking
**Rule:** The best place to look for secrets is where no one else is looking. Filter for fields that matter but haven't been standardized/institutionalized. We know more about faraway stars than human nutrition.

**Test:** Is this domain prestige-rewarded inside academia/consulting? If yes, the secrets have been mined. Hunt the unprestigious-but-load-bearing.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.8 (pp.98-99)

**Chapter:** Ch.8

### Four Reasons Nobody Looks for Secrets
**Rule:** Belief in secrets has been hollowed out by four trends: incrementalism (school rewards small steps), risk aversion (lonely-and-wrong is unbearable), complacency (elites collect rents), flatness (someone smarter would have found it). Each reason for absence is itself why secrets remain.

**Test:** Which of the four headwinds is keeping competitors out of this question? If none — i.e., it's a fashionable problem — there is no secret here.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.8 (pp.91-93)

**Chapter:** Ch.8

### Atoms Over Bits
**Rule:** We were promised flying cars, instead we got 140 characters. Stagnation has compounded in physical-world domains; that's where pricing power has built up.

**Test:** Bits-only plays must justify why software is the binding constraint, not the easy one.

**Source:** Founders Fund manifesto; The End of the Future, 2011

**Chapter:** FF / 2011

### Monopoly-Shaped, Small-Market Start
**Rule:** Real monopolies own >50% of a tiny defined market within a short horizon, then expand concentrically. Last mover, not first mover.

**Test:** Reject 'the X for Y' and '$N billion TAM'. Ask: what does the steady-state monopoly look like in 10 years, and why do you end up holding it?

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Small-Network Paradox
**Rule:** Network-effects businesses must be valuable to the very first users when the network is necessarily small. Xanadu (1960) failed because its tech only worked at scale.

**Test:** Is our product valuable to user #10, not just user #10,000? If the value curve only kicks in at scale, the business is a Xanadu.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5 (p.49)

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Initial Market Must Look Too Small
**Rule:** Successful network businesses' initial markets are 'so small that they often don't even appear to be business opportunities at all.' If MBAs would invest in the v1 market, it's the wrong v1 market.

**Test:** Would a McKinsey TAM slide make our seed market look like a business? If yes, the market is too obvious — and therefore too contested.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5 (p.49)

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Start Small, Expand Concentrically
**Rule:** Always err on the side of starting too small. Then expand to the most-similar adjacent market (Amazon: books → CDs/videos/software → general store; eBay: Beanie Babies → all collectibles). Sequencing is underrated.

**Test:** Have we named the niche we will own first AND the next two concentric rings? Skipping rings = leaping.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5 (pp.52-53)

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Anti-Disruption / Anti-First-Mover-Fetish
**Rule:** Reject 'disruption' framing — it forces you to see yourself through incumbents' eyes (Napster). First-mover is a tactic; it's much better to be the LAST mover. Study the endgame first.

**Test:** Can we describe the company without naming an incumbent we are killing? Can we describe the endgame state — the last great development in this market — and our position in it?

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5 (pp.54-56)

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Definite Optimist Quadrant
**Rule:** Of the four quadrants (US 1950s-60s = definite optimist; US post-1982 = indefinite optimist; Europe = indefinite pessimist; China = definite pessimist), only definite optimism builds new things. Indefinite optimism is inherently unsustainable.

**Test:** Do we have a concrete multi-year plan for a specific better future, or are we hedging optionality? Optionality without conviction is the post-1982 trap.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.6 (pp.61-69)

**Chapter:** Ch.6

### The Founding Moment Is Singular
**Rule:** The founding moment happens once — only at the very start can you set rules that align people toward future value creation. After that, you can extend the founding (by continuing to invent) but you cannot recreate it.

**Test:** Before adding a co-founder, board member, or first 5 hires, ask: 'If I cannot undo this for 7 years, do I still want it?' If the answer requires hedging, defer.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.9 (p.115)

**Chapter:** Ch.9

### Cult-Side of the Spectrum
**Rule:** Plot culture from consultants (nihilism) to cults (dogmatism). Best startups are 'slightly less extreme cults' — fanatically RIGHT about something outsiders missed. If conventional professionals find your company sensible, it isn't a 0-to-1 company.

**Test:** When you describe the company to a sane MBA/consultant, does she nod approvingly? If yes, you're in nihilism territory. If she thinks you're crazy AND you can articulate why she's wrong about a specific secret — right side.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.10 (pp.125-126)

**Chapter:** Ch.10

### Map Yourself to a Channel by Price
**Rule:** Distribution is keyed to deal size: Viral (~$1 CAC, consumer) → Marketing (~$100) → Dead Zone → Personal Sales ($10K-$100K) → Complex Sales ($100K-$10M+, big-co/government). Price-point dictates the only channel that can possibly work.

**Test:** Take your average expected ACV and locate it on the continuum. If you're $1K ACV trying personal sales, or $1M ACV running Facebook ads, you've already lost.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11 (pp.131-132)

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### Computers as Complements, Not Substitutes
**Rule:** The most valuable businesses of the coming decades will empower people, not make them obsolete. Pick problems where humans (intentionality, judgment) and machines (data processing) are categorically different — and design products that fuse the two.

**Test:** Can you state in one sentence the irreducible human judgment your product preserves and amplifies? If your pitch is 'we automate away X', flip it: 'how do we let X-experts do 10x more?' If you can't reframe it that way, problem mis-specified.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.12 (pp.143-145)

**Chapter:** Ch.12

### Globalization vs Technology Quadrant
**Rule:** Globalization (other humans) creates substitutive competition for jobs and resources. Technology (better computers) creates complementary leverage where machines don't demand: all value goes to people. Build in the technology quadrant.

**Test:** On a 2x2 of supply (labor) vs demand (resources) crossed with globalization vs technology — which cell does your business occupy? Globalization-row = competitive trap, not monopoly path.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.12 (pp.132-134)

**Chapter:** Ch.12

### Q2 — The Timing Question
**Rule:** Is now the right time? Not every market is on a Moore's-law curve. Photovoltaic efficiency improved linearly over 50 years. Entering a slow-moving market is fine only if you have a definite plan to take it over.

**Test:** Plot the underlying technology's improvement curve over 30 years. Exponential or linear? If linear, what specific event in the next 24 months breaks the linearity? If you can't name it, your timing thesis is hope, not plan.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.156-157)

**Chapter:** Ch.13

### Q3 — The Monopoly Question
**Rule:** Are you starting with a big share of a small market? You can't dominate a submarket if it's fictional, and huge markets are highly competitive. Trillion-dollar markets mean ruthless, bloody competition.

**Test:** Define the smallest real market in which you can be >50% within 24 months. Is that market actually demarcated by customer type and willingness-to-pay, or is it a slide-deck slice?

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.158-160)

**Chapter:** Ch.13

### Q7 — The Secret Question
**Rule:** Have you identified a unique opportunity others don't see? Every dead cleantech company justified itself with conventional truths everybody agreed on. Whatever is good enough to receive applause from all audiences can only be conventional.

**Test:** State the contrarian claim your business is built on, in one sentence, such that a smart sector-insider would currently disagree. If everyone in the room nods, you have consensus, not a secret.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.166, 173)

**Chapter:** Ch.13

### The Social Entrepreneurship Trap
**Rule:** Solving 'a problem the world cares about' is not a good business if you can't answer 7/7. Whatever is good enough to receive applause from all audiences can only be conventional. Best problems are often the ones nobody else even tries to solve.

**Test:** Does your founding pitch foreground social good more than commercial secret? If both philanthropists and VCs would nod, you're conventional in both directions. Right problems get one nod and one frown.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.166-167)

**Chapter:** Ch.13

### Counter-Positioning
**Rule:** The strongest moat is one the incumbent's own P&L structure prevents them from copying — Anduril vs Lockheed cost-plus, Palantir vs Booz Allen body-shop, SpaceX vs ULA.

**Test:** If a top consultancy or BigTech could clone this tomorrow, the moat is fake. Name what specifically about the incumbent's P&L, union contracts, or channel conflicts prevents copying.

**Source:** Founders Fund revealed portfolio; Competition is for Losers

**Chapter:** FF / WSJ 2014

### Regulated/Mission-Critical Wedge
**Rule:** Regulatory pain is a feature once cleared. A specific buyer for whom this is a top-three problem this quarter beats a generic TAM.

**Test:** Can you name the buyer and their crisis? Palantir/CIA, Anduril/DoD, Oklo/NRC are the templates.

**Source:** Operating history — Palantir founding

**Chapter:** Operating

### Anti-Antichrist Filter (2024+)
**Rule:** Does the company increase exit and optionality (jurisdictional, technological, biological), or does it increase legibility to a central regulator?

**Test:** Global-coordination businesses (carbon registries, AI-safety institutes, CBDCs, ESG compliance) are shorts, not opportunities.

**Source:** Antichrist lectures, 2024-25; Hoover Apocalypse Now Pt I & II

**Chapter:** 2024+

### Reject Indeterminate Macro Bets
**Rule:** A company that needs a specific macro regime to hold is not a company.

**Test:** If success requires peak-oil, dollar-collapse, perpetual ZIRP, etc., discount heavily. Clarium lesson.

**Source:** Operating history — Clarium unwind

**Chapter:** Operating

### Credentialism As Anti-Signal
**Rule:** Pedigree-stacked founding team with no domain obsession is a red flag.

**Test:** Of the first ten hires, how many have a credential-shaped resume vs a project-shaped one? Heavy on the former is yellow.

**Source:** PayPal hiring; Thiel Fellowship Q&A

**Chapter:** Operating

### Build for Takeoff, Not Plateau
**Rule:** Bostrom's four futures: recurrent collapse, plateau, extinction, takeoff. Without new technology, stagnation erupts into conflict. Only takeoff justifies definite long-horizon investment — and it won't happen on its own.

**Test:** If your company succeeds maximally, does the world get a new capability that didn't exist (takeoff contribution), or does it just reshuffle existing capacity (plateau)? Plateau businesses can be profitable but don't merit a founder's life.

**Source:** Zero to One, Conclusion (pp.227-232)

**Chapter:** Conclusion

### Technology as Moral Imperative
**Rule:** We cannot take for granted that the future will be better. New technology is not a preference — it's the only path that prevents stagnation from collapsing into conflict.

**Test:** Could the problem you're working on plausibly contribute to the singular/0-to-1 future? Would a smart person in 2050 say this work mattered for whether humanity took off?

**Source:** Zero to One, Conclusion (pp.232-233)

**Chapter:** Conclusion

## Approach Taste
*Given the problem, which angle to attack from*

Once the problem is right, the playbook is operationally narrow: definite plan, weird small team, full-stack ownership, force-funded by a serious customer, sold by a hidden distribution mechanism. Every doctrine here has a mechanism — not a disposition.

### Determinate Optimism / Master Plan
**Rule:** A bad plan is better than no plan. Lean iteration without vision is a counsel of despair.

**Test:** Founder must articulate a multi-year sequenced plan, even if details are wrong. 'We'll see what the market says' = downgrade.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.6; Cowen 2015

**Chapter:** Ch.6

### Lean Startup as Local-Maximum Trap
**Rule:** Leanness is a methodology, not a goal. MVP iteration finds local maxima; it cannot get you from 0 to 1. In startups, intelligent design works best.

**Test:** Are we iterating toward a pre-articulated end-state, or iterating because we don't know the end-state? The latter is indefinite optimism in costume.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.6 (pp.66-67)

**Chapter:** Ch.6

### Avoid the Indefinite-Finance Trap
**Rule:** Finance, law, PE, management consulting, biotech are indefinite-optimist refuges for high-achieving optionality chasers. Money preferred to anything you'd do with it = indefinite. Money as means to a definite end = sound.

**Test:** Are our hires (and we ourselves) optimizing for optionality or for a specific built thing? Optionality-chasers will not ship a 0-to-1.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.6 (pp.62-63, 70)

**Chapter:** Ch.6

### Invert the Four Dot-Com Dogmas
**Rule:** The post-2000 'lessons' are mistaken reactions. Inversions: bold > trivial; bad plan > no plan; competitive markets destroy profits; sales matters as much as product.

**Test:** When a founder defends a tactic with a dot-com lesson (incrementalism, lean/unplanned, just-improve-on-competition, product-not-sales), probe whether they have actually thought it through or are echoing post-crash dogma.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.2 (pp.20-21)

**Chapter:** Ch.2

### Think-For-Yourself Contrarianism
**Rule:** The most contrarian thing of all is not to oppose the crowd but to think for yourself. Reverse-contrarians (anti-Lean cargo cults, anti-MVP posturing) are still crowd-shaped.

**Test:** Does the founder hold this view because the crowd hates it, or because they reasoned to it from first principles?

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.2 (p.23)

**Chapter:** Ch.2

### Detect the Union-of-Markets Dodge
**Rule:** Monopolists disguise dominance by framing their market as a UNION of large markets ('search ∪ mobile ∪ wearables'). Non-monopolists exaggerate uniqueness via INTERSECTIONS of small markets ('British food ∩ restaurant ∩ Palo Alto').

**Test:** Force the founder to state market size two ways: smallest defensible niche they actually own, broadest plausible category they're part of. The gap reveals which lie they're tempted to tell.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.3 (pp.30-32)

**Chapter:** Ch.3

### Shakespeare-Over-Marx Conflict Frame
**Rule:** Most business conflicts follow Shakespeare, not Marx: combatants are nearly identical and forget why they fight (Gates/Schmidt, Ellison/Siebel, the Square/NetSecure card-reader shape war).

**Test:** Map the founder's stated rivals. If the rivalry is between near-clones obsessed with each other's moves rather than the customer, the company is in a Shakespearean trap.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.4 (pp.40-43)

**Chapter:** Ch.4

### Asperger's Advantage Heuristic
**Rule:** Founders less sensitive to social cues are less likely to do what everyone else is doing, less afraid to pursue obsessions, less likely to abandon convictions in crowds.

**Test:** Weight low-social-conformity positively. A founder who has been visibly indifferent to peer prestige (consulting, banking, FAANG) for years is more likely to escape the rivalry trap.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.4 (p.41)

**Chapter:** Ch.4

### Four Monopoly Characteristics
**Rule:** (1) Proprietary tech 10x better on a load-bearing dimension. (2) Network effects valuable from a small first network. (3) Software-like marginal economics. (4) Earned brand.

**Test:** Score every candidate on all four. Missing the 10x bar is usually fatal.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### 10x Rule on a Dimension That Matters
**Rule:** Proprietary tech must be at least 10x better than its closest substitute on some important dimension. 20% better in the lab won't survive real-world friction.

**Test:** Name the single dimension on which we are 10x better and the substitute we are 10x better than. If we cannot articulate both crisply, we are marginal.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5 (p.48)

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Three Routes to 10x — Invent, Improve, Integrate
**Rule:** Three ways to manufacture a 10x edge: (1) invent something new (PayPal: instant settlement vs 7-10 day check), (2) radically improve existing solution, (3) deliver superior integrated design (iPad making tablets useful). Pick the lane explicitly.

**Test:** Which of the three are we executing? If we cannot name it, we are doing 1.2x cosmetic work.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5 (pp.48-49)

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Software-Shaped Marginal Cost Test
**Rule:** A good startup should have the potential for great scale built into its first design. Service businesses (yoga studios) are structurally bad monopolies because labor is the marginal cost.

**Test:** What is the marginal cost of customer N+1? If it scales with headcount or physical assets, this is a service, not a monopoly.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5 (pp.49-50)

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Substance Precedes Brand
**Rule:** No technology company can be built on branding alone. Apple's brand reinforces a stack of monopolistic advantages. Yahoo!'s 'people then products then traffic then revenue' is reverse-causal.

**Test:** If we removed our brand assets tomorrow, would the underlying tech, scale, or network still constitute a moat? If not, we are running the Yahoo! play.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.5 (pp.50-51)

**Chapter:** Ch.5

### Foundations Are Unfixable
**Rule:** A startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed.

**Test:** Co-founder fit, equity splits, mission, ownership/control/management cleanly defined; board ≤3, ideally odd; full-time only.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.9

**Chapter:** Ch.9

### Ownership-Possession-Control Triad
**Rule:** Three distinct things must be tracked separately: ownership (legal equity), possession (day-to-day operation), control (formal governance, board). Misalignment between any two is the structural cause of most startup blowups.

**Test:** Can you name, for each of the three, who holds it and where their incentives diverge from yours over a 5-year horizon? If you can't draw the triad cleanly today, your foundation is already cracked.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.9 (pp.105-106)

**Chapter:** Ch.9

### On-the-Bus Binary Rule
**Rule:** The decision to involve someone is binary — full-time, equity-bearing, in-room — or external (lawyer/accountant). No part-timers, no consultants, no remote-only for the core.

**Test:** Does this person own stock options AND draw a regular salary AND sit in the room every day? If any of the three is no, they are off the bus — do not let them touch product, customers, or hiring.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.9 (p.110)

**Chapter:** Ch.9

### Equity Asymmetry Is a Feature
**Rule:** Equal share splits among non-founders are a mistake — different talents, opportunity costs, and risk levels demand different grants. But unequal grants must remain confidential.

**Test:** Are early grants differentiated by risk-taken and role-criticality? Is the cap table held closely (not hidden, but not broadcast)? Resentment, not dilution, is the killer.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.9 (p.113)

**Chapter:** Ch.9

### Equity Self-Selection Filter
**Rule:** Anyone who prefers cash over equity is revealing short-term commitment. Thiel cites a PayPal BD consultant who refused options for $5K/day cash — automatic disqualifier.

**Test:** Offer the candidate a credible cash-vs-equity tradeoff (e.g. -20% salary for +2x equity). Watch which way they lean. The lean is the hire decision.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.9 (pp.113-114)

**Chapter:** Ch.9

### Hire Weird; Pay In Equity
**Rule:** First ten hires should look project-shaped, not credential-shaped. Below-market cash + above-market equity sorts and aligns.

**Test:** Founders paying themselves market salary at seed are buying optionality the company cannot afford.

**Source:** Early PayPal hiring & comp

**Chapter:** Operating

### Conspiracy, Not Company
**Rule:** 4-12 high-trust people who can repeat the non-consensus thesis unprompted.

**Test:** Mission-as-vibes ≠ conspiracy. Distributed-by-default with no shared model fails.

**Source:** PayPal war room; Eric Weinstein/The Portal 2019

**Chapter:** Operating

### Culture-as-Conspiracy (No HR Outsourcing)
**Rule:** Recruiting is a core competency — never outsource it. Distinguishing question: not 'why is our mission important?' but 'why would the 20th employee join us instead of Google?'

**Test:** Write down two answers: (1) why your mission is uniquely undone by anyone else, (2) why this team is a unique personal match for this candidate. If either is portable, you don't have a recruiting story.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.10 (pp.118-120)

**Chapter:** Ch.10

### Different in the Same Way
**Rule:** Outwardly: every early hire should be a tribe member — same non-consensus thesis, same obsession. Levchin: make early staff as personally similar as possible. Diversity of background fine; diversity of obsession fatal at <20 people.

**Test:** Name the single non-consensus belief every early employee shares. If you can't, you have a workforce, not a mafia.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.10 (pp.122-123)

**Chapter:** Ch.10

### One Person, One Thing
**Rule:** Inwardly: every employee owns exactly ONE distinct thing and is evaluated only on it. Eliminates internal mimetic competition. Internal conflict is autoimmune disease.

**Test:** For every team member, write a one-sentence unique responsibility. If two sentences overlap, one of those people will leave or both will underperform. Fix overlap before it festers.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.10 (p.124)

**Chapter:** Ch.10

### The Founder Paradox
**Rule:** Strange, extreme, monomaniacal, slightly sociopathic founders outperform.

**Test:** If the CEO would obviously be employable at McKinsey or Google, lower conviction. Look for a burned Plan B.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.14; Oxford Union 2023

**Chapter:** Ch.14

### Inverse-Normal Trait Distribution
**Rule:** Founders' traits don't follow a bell curve — they follow a U-shape. Almost all successful founders are simultaneously insiders and outsiders. Hughes, Lady Gaga, Gates, Parker, Jobs — all extreme on multiple dimensions at once.

**Test:** On the trait axes (insider/outsider, charismatic/abrasive, conventional/eccentric), where does the founder sit? If they cluster at average on all of them, they probably can't pull a company through 0-to-1.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.14 (pp.184-187, 195, 203-205)

**Chapter:** Ch.14

### Vertical Integration Default
**Rule:** Own the stack from atoms to outcome. SpaceX engines + avionics + launch; Anduril hardware + Lattice; Palantir forward-deployed engineers.

**Test:** Thin-API plays in deep tech fail the moat test. Does the company own from physics to customer outcome?

**Source:** Founders Fund portfolio pattern

**Chapter:** FF

### The Palantir Shape (Hybrid Decision-Support)
**Rule:** Software analyzes, trained humans review. PayPal's Igor fraud system worked where pure-automation failed. Target problems with adaptive adversaries, ambiguous signals, or high-stakes decisions where being wrong is catastrophic.

**Test:** Does your product have a 'final mile' where a trained human decides? If your architecture diagram has no human-in-the-loop, competitors with humans-in-the-loop will outperform you on the edge cases that matter.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.12 (pp.135-137)

**Chapter:** Ch.12

### Big Data Is Usually Dumb Data
**Rule:** Computers can find patterns that elude humans, but they don't know how to compare patterns or interpret complex behaviors. Pure ML / pure data-mining startups without designed human-judgment loops will fail on interpretation.

**Test:** Strip the ML out of your pitch. Is there still a business? If the entire value prop is 'we have more data / better model', you're solving for what computers do alone.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.12 (pp.139-140)

**Chapter:** Ch.12

### Sales Is Hidden
**Rule:** Superior sales and distribution by itself can create a monopoly, even with no product differentiation. The converse is not true.

**Test:** Engineering-only founding team with no go-to-market thinker = downgrade regardless of product strength.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### Distribution Power Law
**Rule:** Pick one channel and dominate it. Startups die in the dead zone between channels.

**Test:** Does the founder know the exact graph where viral mechanics will bend? Generic 'we'll do growth marketing' fails.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11; PayPal/eBay bounty math

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### CLV > CAC Is a Hard Rule
**Rule:** Customer Lifetime Value must exceed Customer Acquisition Cost — a literal limit, not aspiration. Higher price = more spend per sale.

**Test:** Compute average CAC and average CLV before scaling any channel. If CLV/CAC < 1, the channel is forbidden — no amount of optimization saves it.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11 (p.131)

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### Complex Sales Means No Salesmen
**Rule:** At seven-figure ACVs, the buyer wants the CEO. Palantir has no dedicated sales team; Karp spends 25 days/month on the road. SpaceX's NASA contracts required Musk personally. Realistic ceiling = 50-100% YoY for a decade.

**Test:** If ACV >$100K: is the CEO doing the selling? Are you modeling 50-100% YoY (not 10x)? Are you starting with a small reference customer before chasing the whale?

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11 (pp.132-134)

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### Viral Means the Product IS the Distribution
**Rule:** Viral only counts if core product use causes a new user to invite another. PayPal: $20 bounty, 7% daily growth. Then targeted highest-velocity niche first (eBay PowerSellers, n=20K).

**Test:** Does sending/sharing the product invite another user as a side-effect of the core action? AND have you identified the smallest viral-niche to dominate first, not the biggest market?

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11 (pp.137-139)

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### Sell To A Forced Buyer First
**Rule:** The early customer who pays for capability, not price, funds the hard R&D.

**Test:** DoD, intelligence community, critical infra, regulated incumbents in crisis. Then expand.

**Source:** Palantir / In-Q-Tel; SpaceX / NASA COTS

**Chapter:** Operating

### Pivot Product, Not Problem
**Rule:** Confinity dropped Palm Pilot for email payments — same problem, new artifact.

**Test:** Teams that hop problems are signaling taste failure upstream.

**Source:** Operating history — PayPal pivot

**Chapter:** Operating

### Q1 — Engineering: 10x or Bust
**Rule:** Can you create breakthrough technology instead of incremental improvements? 10x or it's a feature, not a technology company. Solyndra's cylindrical cells were ~1/π as efficient as flat ones.

**Test:** Name the nearest substitute and the dimension of comparison. If your product isn't 10x better on that dimension, it's a feature.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.154-155)

**Chapter:** Ch.13

### Q4 — People: No Suits
**Rule:** Founders Fund's blanket rule: never invest in a tech CEO who wears a suit. Cleantech failed because it was run by salesman-executives good at raising capital but bad at building products.

**Test:** Sartorial and substantive: does the CEO build/code/operate, or only present? Can the technical co-founders explain the engineering bottleneck in 60 seconds without buzzwords?

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.161-162)

**Chapter:** Ch.13

### Q5 — Distribution: Plan to Deliver
**Rule:** Better Place spent $800M and sold ~1,000 cars because customers had to seek approval, prove they lived near a swap station, sign a fueling subscription, adopt new behaviors before they could buy.

**Test:** Walk a real prospective customer through the buying and onboarding flow end-to-end. Count the friction steps. If acquiring requires belief in your worldview before value is delivered, distribution will kill you.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.163-164)

## Stopping Taste
*When to abandon, when to push, when 'done enough'*

The layer most founders and investors get wrong, and where the revealed record (PayPal regret, Facebook regret, Palantir 17-year hold, Clarium unwind) is the actual data. Hold winners; trim losers; revise positions, not frameworks; never run a portfolio life.

### Power-Law Concentration
**Rule:** The best investment in a fund equals or beats the rest combined. Same is true of a career.

**Test:** Don't diversify like a portfolio. Trim losers, never trim outliers.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.7

**Chapter:** Ch.7

### Fund-Returner Rule
**Rule:** Two strange rules: (1) only invest in companies that could return the entire fund; (2) because rule one is so restrictive, there can be no other rules. Founders Fund: Facebook returned more than the rest of its 2005 fund combined.

**Test:** Could this single bet plausibly return the entire portfolio (or, for a founder, justify a whole career)? If not, it's a lottery ticket.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.7 (pp.74-76)

**Chapter:** Ch.7

### Life Is Not a Portfolio
**Rule:** Life is not a portfolio: not for a startup founder, and not for any individual. You cannot diversify your own life by 'keeping dozens of equally possible careers in ready reserve.' Power-law thinkers hesitate on starting — joining the best rocket while it grows often dominates.

**Test:** Have we picked the one career bet (found OR join the best rocket)? Hedging across 3 'options' is the schoolroom default and it's wrong.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.7 (pp.81-83)

**Chapter:** Ch.7

### Hold The Monopoly
**Rule:** Selling PayPal was the single worst business decision I ever made. If durable pricing power and the moat is widening, default is hold.

**Test:** Liquidity ≠ thesis validation. Selling requires affirmative reason — ceiling, not fatigue.

**Source:** Cowen 2015; Joe Rogan

**Chapter:** Operating

### The Zuckerberg Refusal
**Rule:** When Yahoo! offered $1B for Facebook (2006), Zuckerberg ended the board meeting in 10 minutes: 'We're obviously not going to sell here.' Acquisition offers are signal — if the offer feels generous, the acquirer probably underestimated the plan.

**Test:** Do we have a concrete vision the acquirer cannot price? If we'd seriously consider the offer, our own plan is too thin.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.6 (p.69)

**Chapter:** Ch.6

### Stay Private While Structure Rewards It
**Rule:** Palantir, 2003-2020. Long sales cycles, sensitive customers, R&D depth = reasons to stay private.

**Test:** IPO pressure is usually a tell on bad investors, not a milestone.

**Source:** Operating history — Palantir

**Chapter:** Operating

### Decade-Long Conviction Tolerance
**Rule:** It took us a decade before anyone outside government even understood what Palantir did.

**Test:** If the thesis requires 7-10 years of being misunderstood, that's a feature. Pressure to pivot to legible categories is the failure mode.

**Source:** COSM 2021; Hoover Institution

**Chapter:** Operating

### Escape Velocity = Self-Funding
**Rule:** A company that can survive a 24-month capital winter at current burn dilutes less.

**Test:** Can the company fund the next stage from customer revenue or strategic balance sheet? If not, but moat is real, concentrate harder.

**Source:** Founders Fund deployment pattern

**Chapter:** FF

### Indefinite Pessimism = Abandon
**Rule:** When a founder loses determinate vision and shifts to 'we'll see what the market says,' the company is effectively over.

**Test:** Stop deploying capital. The trigger is a vision shift, not a metrics dip.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.6

**Chapter:** Ch.6

### Revise Positions, Sharpen Frameworks
**Rule:** Clarium taught the discipline of absorbing a wrong position without breaking the framework.

**Test:** Healthy operators revise positions; unhealthy ones revise frameworks under pressure.

**Source:** Operating history — Clarium unwind

**Chapter:** Operating

### Pass On Good-Not-Great
**Rule:** Terminal market share of 15-30% = pass. The thesis only works on 60%+ outcomes.

**Test:** If the honest answer is 'this is a nice business,' the answer is no.

**Source:** Founders Fund revealed pass criteria

**Chapter:** FF

### Stop Fighting Unwinnable Board Fights
**Rule:** When a founder's control is structurally locked, marginal influence < marginal time cost.

**Test:** Shift from active to advisory. Hanging on past that point is ego, not stewardship.

**Source:** Facebook board posture post-2012

**Chapter:** Operating

### Restaurant-Economics Stop Rule
**Rule:** In low-margin competitive ecosystems, every ounce of slack is consumed by survival. Bernard Loiseau's suicide after a guide downgrade illustrates the terminal logic: ruthlessness or death.

**Test:** If the company couldn't survive a single competitor's price cut, or leadership cannot afford a quarter thinking about anything but this quarter, the business is in a ruthlessness-or-death attractor — shut it down or pivot.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.3 (pp.33-34)

**Chapter:** Ch.3

### Pareto/Tolstoy Failure Diagnostic
**Rule:** All happy companies are different: each one earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem. All failed companies are the same: they failed to escape competition.

**Test:** When diagnosing a failing portfolio company, the first question is 'did they ever escape competition?' If no, all other diagnoses are downstream noise.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.3 (p.35)

**Chapter:** Ch.3

### Hamlet/Eggshell Honor Trap
**Rule:** Pride and honor cause founders to fight for stakes 'as thin as an eggshell.' Anyone fights for things that matter; only fools fight for things that don't.

**Test:** Strip the rivalry of honor: would the founder still fight if there were no audience? If the only reason the war continues is reputational, force a merge, exit, or ceasefire (PayPal/X.com 50-50 merger).

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.4 (pp.45-46)

**Chapter:** Ch.4

### Fight Hard or Don't Throw Punches
**Rule:** There is no middle ground: either don't throw any punches, or strike hard and end it quickly. Half-fights drain the company.

**Test:** If engaged in a competitive skirmish (feature-matching, price wars, copycat shapes), force a binary: commit fully to ending the rival, or disengage entirely. Ongoing low-grade rivalry is the worst attractor.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.4 (p.45)

**Chapter:** Ch.4

### Tell a Few, Never Everyone
**Rule:** Unless you have perfectly conventional beliefs, it's rarely a good idea to tell everybody everything. The golden mean between telling nobody and telling everybody is a company.

**Test:** Are we recruiting around a shared secret (fellow conspirators) or around perks/title (mercenaries)? Public-facing positioning should not reveal the secret.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.8 (p.103)

**Chapter:** Ch.8

### Board of Three (never above five private)
**Rule:** A board of three is ideal; never exceed five members while private. Each marginal director compounds the probability of a problem director jeopardizing the company.

**Test:** If the board is >5 pre-IPO, refuse the term sheet or restructure before signing.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.9 (pp.106-108)

**Chapter:** Ch.9

### $150K CEO Salary Cap
**Rule:** A venture-backed early-stage CEO must take ≤$150K/yr. Above that, the CEO behaves like a politician defending the status quo, not a founder fixing problems.

**Test:** Compare CEO cash comp to the lowest-paid engineer. If CEO cash is more than ~2-3x and above $150K, the founder is signaling 'extraction over creation' and the cap table will follow.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.9 (pp.111-112)

**Chapter:** Ch.9

### Refuse the Perk War
**Rule:** Anyone more powerfully swayed by free laundry pickup or pet day care is a bad hire. Cover health insurance basics, then compete only on irreplaceable work + unique people.

**Test:** Strip the recruiting page of every perk. Does the offer still convert? If not, you are buying employees, not recruiting them.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.10 (pp.120-121)

**Chapter:** Ch.10

### Avoid the Dead Zone
**Rule:** Products priced ~$1,000 (small-business software canonical) fall between channels: too cheap for a salesperson to chase, too niche for ads. Most SMB tools die here.

**Test:** If your ACV is $500-$5,000 and your customer is small businesses without a viral hook, redesign pricing or product BEFORE launch. The dead zone is a structural killer.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11 (pp.134-135)

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### Power Law of Distribution — Pick One
**Rule:** Distribution follows a power law: ONE channel will dominate; the rest are noise. Kitchen-sink approach (a few salespeople + some ads + a viral feature bolted on) almost always yields zero working channels.

**Test:** Name your single dominant channel and the metric that proves it's working. If you can name two co-equal channels, you haven't yet found one.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11 (pp.140-141)

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### Engineering-Only Founding Teams Underperform
**Rule:** Engineers systematically underrate sales because their work is transparent (works or doesn't) and sales looks like artifice. But sales is hidden by design — every job in distribution has a non-sales title.

**Test:** Among the founders, who would close a $1M enterprise deal or get the company on the front page? If 'nobody' or 'we'll hire someone,' the founding team is structurally incomplete — fix at founding, not at Series A.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.11 (pp.128-130, 142-143)

**Chapter:** Ch.11

### Q6 — Durability: Plan to Be the Last Mover
**Rule:** Will your market position be defensible 10 and 20 years out? Cleantech ignored China's manufacturing and the rise of fracking; both predictable and lethal.

**Test:** Name the specific competitor or technology shift that could wipe you out in 10 years. If you can't name one, you haven't thought hard enough. Then: what specifically prevents that wipeout?

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.164-165)

**Chapter:** Ch.13

### The 7-for-7 Stopping Rule
**Rule:** If you nail all seven of Thiel's questions, you'll master fortune. Even five or six might work. But cleantech founders started with zero good answers.

**Test:** Score the business 0-1 on each of the seven (engineering, timing, monopoly, people, distribution, durability, secret). If sum ≤4, the right action is to fix or pivot, not push harder. Any one of those mistakes is enough to doom your company.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.13 (pp.153, 167-170)

**Chapter:** Ch.13

### Founder Scapegoating Risk
**Rule:** Scapegoats and founders are structurally identical (extreme, contradictory, simultaneously powerful and powerless) — and a founder under siege is the company's mortality clock.

**Test:** When something goes wrong, does the press/board/team converge on a single-person scapegoat narrative? That's the Girardian phase change. If the founder is being mythologized as either savior or villain, plan succession contingencies.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.14 (pp.191-193, 203)

**Chapter:** Ch.14

### CEO Replacement = Stagnation Signal
**Rule:** When boards remove founders for 'professional management' (Apple-Sculley 1985; Yahoo serial CEOs), companies enter relative stagnation. Apple nearly bankrupt by 1997 under credentialed executives.

**Test:** If the board is debating bringing in 'adult supervision', that's a leading indicator of decline, not maturity — unless the founder is genuinely incapacitated. For investors: replacement is a sell signal.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.14 (pp.207-209)

**Chapter:** Ch.14

### Founder's Self-Mythology Bound
**Rule:** Mirror-rule for the founder: the single greatest danger is to become so certain of his own myth that he loses his mind. Equally insidious: a business losing all sense of myth and mistaking disenchantment for wisdom.

**Test:** Two failure modes: (a) founder treats own intuitions as infallible (late-Hughes pattern); (b) company strips out founder mythology in pursuit of professionalism and loses singular vision. Healthy state has both myth and self-skepticism running concurrently.

**Source:** Zero to One, Ch.14 (pp.210-212)

# The Buffett Doctrine

> *"Rule No. 1: never lose money. Rule No. 2: never forget Rule No. 1."*

**About:** A three-layer filter for evaluating businesses worth owning. Distilled from a year-by-year read of the Berkshire annual letters (1965-2024), the Buffett Partnership letters (1957-1969), the Owner's Manual (1996), six decades of AGM Q&A, the operating record (See's 1972, Coke 1988, Salomon 1991, Dexter 1993, USAir 1989, GEICO 1976/1996, IBM 2011/2018, Apple 2016+, BNSF 2009, Japan 2020-2024), Munger's mental-model corpus, and the revealed 2024-2026 portfolio (record cash, Apple trim, Japanese sogo shosha, Occidental, Greg Abel succession). Every doctrine carries a rule, a concrete test, and its source.

---
## Layer 1 — Problem-Selection Taste
*What business is worth owning at all*

The filter is built to reject anything that lacks durable economics. Most businesses look fine on the income statement and disastrous on the second decade. Pick businesses you would be content to own if the stock market closed for ten years, with pricing power that survives an idiot or a brother-in-law at the helm, and capital requirements that don't quietly devour the cash flow GAAP says you earned.

### Circle of Competence

**Rule:** Stay inside the businesses you actually understand. Knowing the perimeter of the circle matters more than the circle's size.

**Test:** In one paragraph, jargon-free, explain how this business earns money in 10 years and what could break it. If you cannot, pass — even when the crowd is making money on it (1999 dot-coms, 2017-2021 crypto).

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 5); Letter 1996; AGM 1999

### Castle and Moat

**Rule:** A great business is an economic castle protected by a moat that widens every year. Without a structural moat, capitalism erodes returns to the cost of capital.

**Test:** Name the moat in five words: brand, switching cost, scale, network, or low-cost producer. If you cannot, it is a commodity in disguise.

**Source:** Letter 1995; Letter 1999; AGM 1999

### Pricing Power Test

**Rule:** The single most important decision in evaluating a business: can it raise prices 10% without losing material volume? If a price increase requires prayer, it is a terrible business.

**Test:** Pull a 5-year price-volume series. Real prices flat or declining = no moat regardless of headline margins.

**Source:** Bloomberg 2011; Letter 1991 (consumer franchise)

### Honest-Idiot Filter

**Rule:** Buy a business so good that an idiot can run it — because sooner or later, one will. Management quality is finite; franchise quality is durable.

**Test:** Imagine a mediocre CEO at the helm for 10 years. Does the business still earn high ROIC? If only a great CEO can save it, you are buying the CEO, not the business.

**Source:** Letter 1988 (Coca-Cola); Owner's Manual 1996

### The Inevitables

**Rule:** A handful of businesses are so dominant their endpoint share is a near-certainty over decades. Buy these on dips even at full prices — and never let the multiple alone push you out.

**Test:** Will this business sell more units 20 years from now to an addiction-grade consumer base? Coke (1996), Gillette (1996), Apple (2016) cleared this bar; IBM (2011) did not.

**Source:** Letter 1996

### Consumer Franchise Triple Test

**Rule:** A consumer franchise has three properties: customers think they need it, no close substitute, no price regulation. Newspapers had all three until they didn't.

**Test:** All three? You probably have a franchise. Two? Vulnerable. One? Commodity. Re-test every five years — moats decay.

**Source:** Letter 1991

### Reject Capital-Hungry Businesses

**Rule:** A business that requires constant reinvestment to maintain unit economics is a gerbil wheel. Reported earnings systematically overstate true earnings via the depreciation-vs-maintenance-capex lie.

**Test:** Compute real maintenance capex (not 'growth capex' marketing). If maintenance capex ≈ depreciation ≈ most of operating cash flow, owner earnings are an illusion.

**Source:** Letter 1985; Letter 1986 (Owner Earnings appendix)

### The Textile Mill Lesson

**Rule:** Pouring capital into a business with poor economics doesn't create a good business; it creates a bigger bad business. The Berkshire Hathaway textile-mill closure (1985) is the canonical reminder.

**Test:** If the rational capital decision is to liquidate, no operational improvement justifies reinvesting. Sentiment, ego, and identity are the trap.

**Source:** Letter 1985 (textile closure); Letter 1989 (Mistakes)

### Owner Earnings, Not Reported Earnings

**Rule:** Owner earnings = reported earnings + non-cash D&A − required maintenance capex − required working capital additions. EBITDA, in Munger's words, 'ignores reality.'

**Test:** Build the owner-earnings bridge for any candidate. If reported earnings >> owner earnings consistently, the GAAP number is fiction.

**Source:** Letter 1986 (Appendix on owner earnings)

### ROIC Above Cost of Capital, Sustained

**Rule:** The single most important fact about a business is the return it earns on invested capital, sustained for decades. A business growing 6% with 6% ROIC is dead money in disguise.

**Test:** 10-year average ROIC > 15%? Then decompose: how much can be reinvested at that rate, and for how long? Runway × rate is the compounding equation.

**Source:** Letter 1979; Munger / Poor Charlie's Almanack

### Three Classes of Business

**Rule:** The best business reinvests large amounts at high returns for a long time. The second best earns lots and pays it out. The worst needs lots and earns little. Almost everything in public markets is the third.

**Test:** Classify the candidate: compounder, dividender, or capital-eater? Capital-eater is auto-reject regardless of accounting earnings.

**Source:** Letter 1992 (Intrinsic Value)

### Avoid The Capital-Hungry, Low-Margin Quadrant

**Rule:** Airlines, autos, mass-market retail, steel, and commodity chemicals collectively destroyed more capital than any sectors of the 20th century. Treat any business with similar economics as guilty until proven innocent.

**Test:** Quadrant the candidate by capital intensity × pricing power. The top-right cell (low capital × high pricing) is the only target.

**Source:** Letter 1996; USAir post-mortem (Letter 1996)

### The Goodwill Discipline

**Rule:** Economic goodwill is real but only worth paying for when it compounds with inflation without reinvestment. This distinguishes See's (real goodwill) from typical M&A goodwill (accounting fiction).

**Test:** Will pricing power offset inflation without absorbing capital? See's: yes. Most acquired goodwill: no — it is a balance-sheet plug for overpayment.

**Source:** Letter 1983 (Goodwill of Businesses appendix)

### See's Pivot — Quality Over Bargain

**Rule:** It is far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Munger pulled WB out of cigar-butt investing with See's in 1972.

**Test:** Would you still own this 20 years out if you paid full price today? If no, you are cigar-butting — and cigar-butts don't scale.

**Source:** Letter 1989 (Mistakes of the First 25 Years); See's acquisition 1972

### Reject the Cigar-Butt

**Rule:** Cigar-butt investing — one last puff from a discarded business — works on small capital and breaks at scale. WB's biggest absolute mistakes (Berkshire textile, Hochschild Kohn, Dexter) were cigar-butts.

**Test:** Is the only bull case 'it's cheap on book / liquidation value' with no franchise? Pass — unless capital is tiny and the runway short.

**Source:** Letter 1989; Letter 1999 (Dexter)

### Demand Earnings Predictability

**Rule:** Stick to businesses whose long-term economics are reasonably predictable. Cyclicals, hot industries, and unproven business models violate this; their DCFs are theatre.

**Test:** Could you write a confident 2046 income statement for this business? If no, your valuation is a Rorschach test.

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 4)

### The Too-Hard Pile

**Rule:** Most decisions belong in the too-hard pile. There is no penalty for missing — strikes are uncalled. The penalty is for swinging at what you don't understand.

**Test:** Must we have an opinion on this? If no, decline. The career imperative to 'have a view' is the mortal enemy of capital.

**Source:** AGM repeatedly; Munger / Almanack

### Hate Forecasting; Love Endurance

**Rule:** Macro forecasts predict nothing reliably enough to bet on. Bet on businesses that survive whatever weather arrives.

**Test:** Does the thesis require a specific macro regime, geopolitical outcome, or rate path? Discount heavily — usually fatally.

**Source:** Letter 1991; Letter 2008

### Float Is the Best Liability

**Rule:** Insurance float is borrowing at less than zero, provided underwriting is profitable. Operating businesses with structurally negative working capital approximate the same trick.

**Test:** Does this business get paid before it pays its costs? Negative working capital is a structural advantage hiding in plain sight (GEICO, Costco, See's).

**Source:** Letter 1990s (float series); GEICO acquisition 1996

### Avoid Adverse Selection

**Rule:** In any business where the counterparty knows more than you — reinsurance, specialty lending, market-making — the smart-looking volume is the worst paper.

**Test:** Who is on the other side, and why are they trading with us? If we cannot answer crisply, we are the patsy.

**Source:** Salomon 1991; Gen Re derivatives 2002 letter; reinsurance taste (Ajit)

### Reject Derivatives and Counterparty Webs

**Rule:** Complex derivative books with off-balance-sheet exposures are unanalyzable. Buffett: 'financial weapons of mass destruction.' Even good operators (LTCM, Bear, Lehman) blow up.

**Test:** Does the balance sheet require footnotes longer than the 10-K to understand? Decline. If counterparty risk is more than ~5% of equity, fragile.

**Source:** Letter 2002 (derivatives WMD)

### Distrust Conglomerate Logic Without Operating Returns

**Rule:** Diversification by acquisition without owner-earnings discipline (1960s-70s conglomerates: ITT, Gulf+Western, LTV) destroys value via empire-building. Berkshire works because Buffett doesn't 'run' anything operationally.

**Test:** Has the holding-co's allocator beaten S&P over 10 years after fees? If no, the conglomerate structure is the cost, not the moat.

**Source:** Letter 1981 (Toad-Princess theory)

### The Toad-Princess Trap

**Rule:** Most CEOs justify expensive acquisitions by hoping their kiss will turn the toad into a princess. The kiss almost always fails. Buffett's exceptions (See's, GEICO, BNSF) were not toads to begin with.

**Test:** Is the target already great, or does it need fixing? 'Fix-it' deals are toad kisses, regardless of synergy slides.

**Source:** Letter 1981

### Local Monopolies Aren't Forever

**Rule:** Even apparently bulletproof franchises decay when distribution shifts. Newspapers were Inevitables in 1991 and obituaries by 2009. A moat eaten by technology was never really a moat.

**Test:** What technology shift would dissolve this moat in 15 years? If you cannot name one, you haven't earned the position.

**Source:** Letters 2009-2020 (newspapers); Buffalo News divestment 2020

### The American Tailwind

**Rule:** Buffett's revealed bias: bet with US capitalism. $1 invested in S&P in 1942 grew to >$5,288 by 2018. Short-the-economy theses are losing trades over multi-decade horizons.

**Test:** Is the business participating in the long secular tailwind, or fighting it (regulatory, demographic, productivity)? Fighting the tailwind needs an extraordinary moat to compensate.

**Source:** Letter 2018 (American Tailwind)

### Show Me the Incentive (Munger)

**Rule:** Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome. Misaligned management, customer, regulator, or distributor incentives are the structural cause of most business failures.

**Test:** Whose incentives have to hold for this thesis to work? If any link is fragile or perverse, downgrade hard.

**Source:** Munger / Poor Charlie's Almanack; AGM repeatedly

### Reject Consensus-Bargain Mediocrity

**Rule:** A business universally seen as a 'bargain' is usually correctly priced for its mediocre economics. Big returns come from being right where others are confused, not from being more careful where everyone agrees.

**Test:** What does the seller know that we don't? On average businesses, markets are roughly efficient. Edge comes from understanding non-average businesses.

**Source:** Letter 1987 (Mr. Market); Letter 1992

### Beware 'It's Different This Time'

**Rule:** Ninety-nine times out of a hundred, 'it's different this time' precedes a regression. The exceptions (Apple's services flip, Japan's 2020 trading houses, GEICO's 1976 turnaround) require concrete identifiable mechanism.

**Test:** Is there a specific mechanism that breaks the prior pattern, or is this hope dressed up as analysis? Demand the mechanism, not the narrative.

**Source:** Letter 2000 (dot-com retrospective); AGM 1999

### The Owner's Mindset

**Rule:** Treat every share as fractional ownership of the underlying business. Mr. Market is a manic-depressive partner whose quotes you may use, not obey.

**Test:** Would you buy the whole business at this implied price? If no, why are you buying 0.001%?

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 1); Letter 1987 (Mr. Market)

### Ten-Year Hold Test

**Rule:** Our favorite holding period is forever. If the business doesn't survive a decade of doing nothing, it isn't a long-duration asset and shouldn't be in the portfolio.

**Test:** Imagine the market closes for 10 years today. Would you still buy this at this price? Yes = qualifies. No = pass or trim.

**Source:** Letter 1988

### One Decade, One Decision (Munger)

**Rule:** A few great decisions over a lifetime drive most of the result. Patience is not a virtue, it is the strategy. Sit on your ass investing.

**Test:** How many real decisions has the operator made in 10 years? Hyperactivity is anti-signal; the great compounders made fewer than five.

**Source:** Munger AGM 2014; Letter 1991

### Catastrophe Survival Is Pre-Condition

**Rule:** Rule 1: never lose money. Rule 2: never forget Rule 1. Survival precedes optimization. A 50% drawdown requires a 100% recovery.

**Test:** What is the worst-case permanent capital impairment if the bear case hits? If > 30% of equity, position size or pass.

**Source:** Letter 1992; AGM repeatedly

### Speculation Is Not Investment

**Rule:** Investment requires productive cash flows. Speculation requires a greater fool. Conflating the two is the most common source of permanent loss in retail capital.

**Test:** Strip out price appreciation. Does the asset produce value in any future state? If no — gold bars, crypto, NFTs, baseball cards — it is a chain letter, not a holding.

**Source:** Letter 2011 (gold/farmland/business comparison); AGM 2018 (crypto)

### Look-Through Earnings

**Rule:** Owners of a stake in a business own its retained earnings whether distributed or not. A high-ROIC company retaining and compounding is more valuable than the dividend it doesn't pay.

**Test:** Compute look-through earnings: your share of underlying owner earnings, not just dividends received. If the gap is large and the retention is reinvested at high ROIC, do not be fooled by the lower headline yield.

**Source:** Letter 1980; Letter 1990 (look-through)

### Reject the Best-Year-Ever Cyclical

**Rule:** When a commodity producer prints its best year ever (oil 2008, shipping 2007, steel 2021, semiconductor capex 2022), the marginal capital is being committed at the cycle peak. Buying at peak earnings × peak multiple is the fastest way to permanent loss.

**Test:** Where is this business in its capital cycle? If recent earnings are >2x the 10-year average and the industry is adding capacity, you are the marginal buyer at the top.

**Source:** Letter 1990 (Wells Fargo opportunistic); commodity-cycle history

## Layer 2 — Approach Taste
*Given the business, how to engage with it*

Once the business is right, the discipline is operationally narrow: pay a price that gives a margin of safety, partner with operators who run it like owners, let cash compound rather than churn, and never delegate the only decision that ultimately matters — capital allocation. Every doctrine here has a mechanism — not a disposition.

### Margin of Safety

**Rule:** Pay materially less than intrinsic value so a wrong assumption doesn't sink the position. The most important three words in investing.

**Test:** At purchase price, what does the business need to earn just to break even on long Treasuries? If the breakeven case requires above-trend execution, there is no margin.

**Source:** Graham 1949 (Intelligent Investor Ch.20); Letter 1983; Letter 2014

### Intrinsic Value as Discounted Owner Earnings

**Rule:** Intrinsic value = present value of owner earnings the business will produce from now until judgment day, discounted at the long Treasury yield. The only sound definition; the inputs are subjective.

**Test:** Sketch a 30-year owner-earnings stream plus terminal value. The point isn't precision; it's discipline to confront the actual cash claim you are buying.

**Source:** Letter 1992; Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 7)

### Mr. Market Discipline

**Rule:** The market exists to serve, not to instruct. Use Mr. Market when his quotes are absurd; ignore him otherwise.

**Test:** Are we deciding because our intrinsic-value sheet diverged from the quote, or because the quote moved? The latter is gambling dressed in spreadsheets.

**Source:** Letter 1987 (Mr. Market); Graham (Intelligent Investor Ch.8)

### Be Greedy When Others Are Fearful

**Rule:** The price you pay is the rate of return. Maximum value comes when consensus loathing or forced-seller mechanics push prices well below intrinsic worth.

**Test:** Are sellers dumping for non-economic reasons (margin calls, mandate, redemption, regulatory, year-end)? That is the buy. Voluntary equilibrium prices rarely offer asymmetry.

**Source:** NYT Op-ed Oct 16, 2008; Letter 2008

### Don't Confuse Activity With Achievement

**Rule:** Hyperactive trading destroys returns via spreads, taxes, and mistakes. Sit on your ass investing. The S&P beats most professional managers by doing nothing for them.

**Test:** Has the position thesis changed, or has only the price? If only the price changed, the right action is usually nothing.

**Source:** Munger AGM repeatedly; Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 11)

### Concentration Within the Circle

**Rule:** Diversification is protection against ignorance; for someone who knows what they're doing, concentration is rational. Top-5 positions should be a meaningful share of portfolio value.

**Test:** Could you stomach 5x the position size if quotes halved? If no, you don't believe enough to own the size you have.

**Source:** Partnership Letter 1965; Letter 1991; Letter 1993

### Twenty-Punch-Card Heuristic

**Rule:** Imagine you have a 20-hole punch card for life-time investments. Every decision permanently consumes one. Most people would punch better holes if they had the card.

**Test:** Is this candidate one of the 20 best you'll ever see? If not, hold cash and wait.

**Source:** Buffett at Wharton 1989; Munger / Almanack

### Cash Is a Call Option on Opportunity

**Rule:** Cash is rarely the highest-yielding asset, but it has option value when prices break. Berkshire ran $325B+ in cash and equivalents by year-end 2024 deliberately.

**Test:** At today's prices, is the marginal dollar best deployed in this candidate, or held as optionality? If unsure, the answer defaults to cash — being patient is free.

**Source:** Letter 2008; Letter 2024

### Pay Up for Quality

**Rule:** A wonderful company at a fair price beats a fair company at a wonderful price. Pay quality multiples for quality businesses; don't insist on Graham-statistical cheap on durable franchises.

**Test:** Is the multiple anchored by ROIC × runway × moat durability, or by hope? Quality bought cheap is a once-a-decade event; quality bought fairly is a once-a-year decision.

**Source:** Letter 1989 (Mistakes); Munger; See's 1972

### Don't Trust the Macro Caller

**Rule:** Even being right on macro doesn't deliver returns; the linkage from forecast to security price is a lottery. Berkshire makes essentially zero macro bets in 60 years.

**Test:** Strip the macro narrative from the thesis. Does the case still hold on business fundamentals alone? If not, the macro was the case — pass.

**Source:** Letter 1991; AGM repeatedly

### Float-Funded Holding Structure

**Rule:** Berkshire works because float (insurance reserves, BNSF deferred taxes) funds equities at sub-zero cost. Operating cash + float > marginal asset price is the structural edge.

**Test:** For an operator candidate, is structural cost-of-funds an advantage, parity, or disadvantage? Disadvantage compounds and eventually buries even great asset selection.

**Source:** Letter 1990s (float series); GEICO 1996

### Buy Whole Businesses Where Possible

**Rule:** Owning 100% removes Mr. Market's noise and gives full control over capital allocation. Berkshire structurally prefers acquisitions over public equities when prices allow.

**Test:** For an allocator candidate, does the model prefer fee businesses or owned cash flows? Fee dependents are fragile to mandate change; owned cash flows are not.

**Source:** Letter 1986; Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 1)

### The Owner's Manual

**Rule:** Berkshire's 14 explicit principles state how the company treats outside owners. Every operator should be auditable against an analogous written document.

**Test:** Is there a durable, written statement of how this management treats outside owners? Without it, governance is ad hoc and will drift toward management's interests under stress.

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996

### Long Tenure for the Operator

**Rule:** Capital allocators improve with reps. Short-tenure operators (5-7 year CEO churn) destroy compounding through reset costs, strategy reversals, and reorg theatre.

**Test:** Does the CEO own meaningful equity bought with personal after-tax cash AND plan to stay? If either is no, governance discount.

**Source:** Buffett 60 years; Munger 49 years at Berkshire

### Skin in the Game, Not Optionality

**Rule:** The CEO should have own-it-with-after-tax-cash exposure, not a bag of options granted by the board. Options reward variance; equity rewards compounding. Different incentives, different outcomes.

**Test:** What % of CEO net worth is in equity bought with personal money? If the answer is 'large grants but no purchases,' the comp is alignment-as-marketing.

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 6); Letter 1991

### Comp Tied to What the CEO Controls

**Rule:** Pay tied to factors the CEO actually controls — operating performance, return on incremental capital — not to share price or peer-group benchmarks. Salomon-style payouts under weak governance produce blowups.

**Test:** Does comp align with multi-year owner earnings, or is it a heads-I-win-tails-shareholders-lose option grant priced at the average?

**Source:** Berkshire comp model; Letter 1991 (Salomon)

### Hire Operators You Don't Need to Manage

**Rule:** Buy businesses run by managers who would run them well even after sale. Berkshire HQ has ~26 people; it adds value by removing meddling, not by adding it.

**Test:** Is the management capable of running this without the CEO/founder for a decade? If only a specific person makes it work, key-person risk is the dominant variable.

**Source:** Letter 1993; Berkshire HQ structure

### Invert, Always Invert (Munger)

**Rule:** Don't ask 'how do I get rich' — ask 'how do I avoid being stupid.' Inverting eliminates more bad outcomes than optimizing produces good ones.

**Test:** List the 5 ways this thesis dies. Have we explicitly defended against each? If three or more lack defense, the thesis is naked.

**Source:** Munger / Almanack; Jacobi via Munger

### Capital Allocation Is the Job

**Rule:** After 10 years, a CEO will personally allocate 60%+ of the company's accumulated capital. Most CEOs were promoted on operating skill and have zero training as allocators.

**Test:** Does the CEO articulate a clear allocator hierarchy (reinvest in high-ROIC organic growth > buyback below intrinsic > dividend > acquisition)? Without that ranking, the default is empire-building.

**Source:** Letter 1987 (Capital Allocation)

### Buyback Discipline

**Rule:** Buybacks are right only when shares trade meaningfully below conservative intrinsic value AND the business has surplus cash. Above intrinsic, buybacks destroy value — most corporate buybacks are timed at peaks.

**Test:** At today's price, is the company buying back? Does management explain the price/value math? Mechanical buybacks regardless of price are an anti-signal.

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 11); Letter 2011; Letter 2018

### Don't Average Down on a Broken Thesis

**Rule:** Averaging down on a price swing with thesis intact is fine; averaging down on a broken thesis doubles the mistake. The threshold is whether the moat survived, not whether the chart is red.

**Test:** Has anything material changed about competitive position, unit economics, or trust? If yes, the right size is different even at lower price — possibly zero.

**Source:** Letter 1993 (distinction); Wells Fargo arc

### Tax-Aware Buy-and-Hold

**Rule:** Deferred capital gains are an interest-free loan from the government. Long-tenure compounding inside a stable structure beats high-turnover by a wider margin than most operators model.

**Test:** For each rotation, does the after-tax IRR of the new position genuinely beat the after-tax IRR of holding? Often it doesn't — even if pre-tax improves.

**Source:** Berkshire structure post-1965; Letter 1989

### Reject Excess Leverage

**Rule:** Leverage works until it doesn't, and when it doesn't, it ends the game. 'If you're smart, you don't need it; if you're dumb, you shouldn't use it.'

**Test:** What happens to equity if assets fall 30%? If the answer is insolvency, leverage is too high regardless of historical comfort.

**Source:** Letter 1990; Letter 2002 (derivatives)

### Position Permanent Capital to Buy the Bottom

**Rule:** Berkshire's best buys came when others were forced sellers (1973-74, 2008-09, 2020). Permanent capital is not just 'no redemptions' — it is the strategic ability to be the buyer no one else can be.

**Test:** In their last drawdown, was the operator a net buyer or a net seller? Forced sellers are eaten by patient capital; the role is permanent and chosen.

**Source:** 1974, 2008-09, 2020 deployments

### Long-Dated Debt Only

**Rule:** Debt should be long-dated, fixed-rate, and covenant-light. Short-dated rolling debt is the structural cause of corporate failure (Bear, Lehman, SVB, every leveraged credit cycle).

**Test:** What is the average maturity of the candidate's debt? If average < 5 years and refi conditions could plausibly close, the business is fragile to a window you don't control.

**Source:** Letter 1989; Letter 2008

### Reinsurance Capital-Cycle Discipline

**Rule:** Underwrite for profit, not premium volume. Walk away from underpriced business even if it means giving up market share for years. Berkshire reinsurance shrank through soft cycles by design.

**Test:** Is the insurer's combined ratio < 100 across the cycle, including soft-market years? If they grew through the soft market, they took adverse-selected paper that detonates later.

**Source:** Letter 1996; Letter 2017; Ajit Jain underwriting record

### Never Lose a Shred of Reputation (Salomon Rule)

**Rule:** Lose money for the firm and I will be understanding; lose a shred of reputation for the firm and I will be ruthless. Trust compounds; betrayal is binary.

**Test:** Does the operator have a single-incident reputation rule? Or do they prosecute 'performance' while letting ethics slide? The latter is a delayed blowup.

**Source:** Buffett to Salomon, Senate testimony 1991

### Use Concentration to Force Honesty

**Rule:** If you make 20 picks, you can be sloppy on each. Concentration is a forcing function for actual analysis.

**Test:** At intended position size, would you be content to be wrong publicly for 3 years? If the bet would be embarrassing, your conviction is performative.

**Source:** Munger AGM repeatedly

### Refuse Career-Risk Trades

**Rule:** The institutional imperative pushes managers to copy peers and chase narrative. The price of being right and lonely is acceptable; the price of being conventionally wrong is not — for Buffett.

**Test:** Is this trade rational because it is right, or because it is defensible? Defensibility is career insurance, not return.

**Source:** Letter 1989 (Institutional Imperative); Letter 1991

### The Institutional Imperative

**Rule:** (1) Resistance to changing direction. (2) Mindless busy-work. (3) Peer copying — any business proposal of a peer will be copied. (4) Dignifying any business proposal of the boss. Almost every CEO is captive to it.

**Test:** Would this decision be made by a single rational owner deploying personal cash? If not, the imperative is at work — and the cost is borne by outside shareholders.

**Source:** Letter 1989 (Mistakes of the First 25 Years)

### Build a Moat With Capital Discipline, Not Marketing

**Rule:** Some moats are inherited (brand). The durable ones are built via cost-structure or distribution choices that compound for decades. GEICO's direct-to-consumer model was a 1936 capital-allocation choice paying dividends 90 years later.

**Test:** Is the moat a function of what the operator spends (defensive) or what they don't spend (structural)? Structural beats defensive over 20 years.

**Source:** GEICO 1936-2024; See's 1972

### The Sokol Rule

**Rule:** Even a hint of self-dealing or front-running by a senior insider is grounds for removal. Reputational integrity > talent.

**Test:** Are there any small lapses being rationalized as 'isolated'? They are not small — they are the visible 5% of an iceberg.

**Source:** Sokol/Lubrizol affair 2011

### Don't Buy Tech You Don't Understand — Until You Do

**Rule:** Buffett famously avoided tech for decades. The Apple position (2016 onward, sourced by Combs/Weschler) was framed as a consumer-products business with switching costs, not a tech bet. The discipline is insisting on understanding the cash flows.

**Test:** Can you describe this tech company in consumer-product terms — recurring use, switching cost, brand pricing power? If not, it is a venture bet, not an investment.

**Source:** Letter 2016 (Apple); 1999 dot-com refusal (Sun Valley speech)

### The Two-List System (Munger)

**Rule:** List the things you want most; circle the top 2 or 3; the bottom of the list is the avoid-at-all-cost list. Diffuse focus is the killer of compounding.

**Test:** Of the businesses in the candidate operator's portfolio, are the 2-3 best ones getting all the marginal capital? Or is there subsidy of weaker units 'because they're already there'?

**Source:** Munger / Almanack

### Reinvest at High ROIC, or Pay It Out

**Rule:** If the business can deploy retained earnings at a return above your alternative, retain. If not, pay it out. The wrong answer is 'retain anyway because the CEO wants a bigger empire.'

**Test:** What is the marginal ROIC on retained earnings over the last 5 years? If it is below 10%, the company should be paying out, not reinvesting — Owner's Manual Principle 9.

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 9); Letter 1984

### Treat Every Decision As If It Were Your Own Money

**Rule:** Outside-money managers diverge from owner behavior under fee pressure, mandate constraint, and quarterly visibility. Berkshire's structure is engineered to remove all three so decisions look identical to a private owner's.

**Test:** Would the operator make this same decision if 100% of the capital and gain/loss were personal? If no, the divergence is the cost of the structure.

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 1); Buffett personal compensation history

## Layer 3 — Stopping Taste
*When to sell, when to hold, when to admit the mistake*

Buffett's revealed record on stopping is the actual data: 60 years at Berkshire, three near-permanent positions (Coca-Cola, American Express, Apple), a small cohort of admitted mistakes (Dexter, USAir, IBM, the airlines in 2020, the 2024 Apple trim), and a structural refusal to retire. The pattern: hold winners until the moat genuinely fades; sell only when the thesis breaks, not when the price moves; never let a wrong position contaminate the framework.

### Hold Winners Forever, Within Reason

**Rule:** Default action on a compounder is hold. Default action on a non-compounder is don't own it.

**Test:** Has anything material about durable competitive advantage changed? If no, hold regardless of multiple expansion. The temptation to 'lock in' is almost always wrong on Inevitables.

**Source:** Letter 1988 ('favorite holding period is forever')

### Sell When the Thesis Breaks, Not When the Price Moves

**Rule:** If the moat erodes, fundamentals deteriorate structurally, or the original reason no longer holds, sell — even at a paper loss. Stock-price moves alone are not signal.

**Test:** Restate your original 5-bullet thesis. How many bullets still hold? Three or fewer = sell, regardless of P&L.

**Source:** Letter 1979; Wells Fargo arc 2016-2020

### Sell to Reallocate, Not to 'Take Profit'

**Rule:** Selling to 'lock in gains' is tax-inefficient and usually wrong. Sell only because the marginal alternative is better risk-adjusted, after tax.

**Test:** Is the new candidate's expected after-tax IRR > continued hold's? If no, holding is correct even after a multi-year run.

**Source:** Owner's Manual 1996 (Principle 11); Letter 1988

### Trim for Position-Sizing, Not for Quality

**Rule:** Even a great business can become an unacceptable position size relative to portfolio risk. Trimming for risk discipline is distinct from a thesis change — and should be communicated as such.

**Test:** Is the trim because the business deteriorated (sell logic) or because the position is now > intended max sizing (risk logic)? Different actions, different signals.

**Source:** Apple trim 2024; Letter 2024

### Admit the Mistake Quickly and Publicly

**Rule:** A confessed mistake is corrected faster. Buffett names the dollar cost, the lesson, and the rule that broke. Berkshire doesn't write down failure quietly; it dissects it in the annual letter.

**Test:** When the operator admits a loss, do they name (a) the structural error, (b) the dollar cost, (c) the rule violated, (d) the prevention going forward? Vague mea culpas predict repetition.

**Source:** Letter 1989 (Mistakes); Letter 1999 (Dexter); Letter 2018 (IBM)

### The Dexter Lesson

**Rule:** Buffett bought Dexter Shoe (1993) for $433M of Berkshire stock; it was worth roughly zero by 2007. A cigar-butt regression — paid full price for a business with no moat, in stock that compounded 10x. Worst dollar cost of any single mistake.

**Test:** Is the candidate a 'good operator in a tough industry'? Tough industries beat good operators most of the time, and paying in undervalued currency multiplies the loss.

**Source:** Letter 1999; Letter 2007

### The USAir Lesson

**Rule:** Buffett bought USAir preferred 1989; coupon was suspended; 'the airline business is a death trap.' Avoid sectors where capital intensity + commodity output + labor leverage compound against the equity holder.

**Test:** Does the business have any of the airline trifecta — capital intensity, commodity pricing, labor leverage? If two of three, treat as USAir until proven otherwise.

**Source:** Letter 1989; Letter 1996 (USAir mark-down)

### The Berkshire Hathaway Itself Mistake

**Rule:** Buffett's worst capital-allocation decision was buying Berkshire Hathaway (the textile mill) in 1962 in a fit of pique over Seabury Stanton's 1/8th-point share-price chiseling. Estimated lifetime opportunity cost: $200B+. Even great allocators can be hijacked by emotion.

**Test:** Are we acting on irritation, vendetta, or sunk cost? Stop. Decisions made in the wrong emotional state always cost more than the dollar amount suggests.

**Source:** HBO Becoming Warren Buffett 2017; Letter 1985

### Walk Away From Investment-Bank-Style Risk

**Rule:** When discovery of misconduct or hidden adverse-selection structure surfaces, the answer is 'out, fast' — not 'let's manage it.' Salomon 1991 nearly killed the firm; Buffett took the CEO seat for 9 months to defuse. The avoidable version is to never enter such structures.

**Test:** At first hint of an integrity issue or hidden book risk, reduce or exit before being forced. Voluntary exits beat forced ones 100% of the time.

**Source:** Salomon 1991; Letter 1991

### The IBM Lesson — Even Great Operators Misjudge

**Rule:** Buffett bought IBM 2011, exited 2018 at meaningful loss. Misread the durability of the franchise vs. the cloud transition. The lesson: a moat in 2010 is not a moat in 2020 if a technology shift is in motion.

**Test:** When you exit, write down what you missed and why. Without an explicit post-mortem, the next IBM is identical in shape — and you will buy it again.

**Source:** Letter 2011 (entry); Letter 2018 (exit)

### Stop Listening to Your Own Brand

**Rule:** Buffett's 'I don't buy tech' became dogma until Munger and the Combs/Weschler team pushed Apple in 2016. The framework should serve outcomes, not vice versa — and the great operator updates the framework when reality forces it.

**Test:** Is your filter rejecting candidates because the rule says no, or because the analysis says no? They sound identical until you check.

**Source:** Apple 2016 reversal; AGM 2017

### Liquidate Operating Businesses With Permanent Decline

**Rule:** When the rational capital-allocation answer is 'shut it down,' emotional attachment is a tax on shareholders. Buffett closed the textile mill — Berkshire's namesake — in 1985 despite founder identity.

**Test:** Is the candidate operator keeping a structurally declining unit alive for narrative reasons? Capital subsidies to dying units = governance discount on the whole company.

**Source:** Letter 1985 (textile closure)

### Do Not Sell Quality on Macro Fear

**Rule:** Buffett added during every major panic — 1973-74, 1987, 1990, 2001, 2008-09, 2020. Selling quality on macro fear locks in opportunity cost; the correct response is usually to add to existing winners.

**Test:** Is the urge to sell driven by news cycle or by changed competitive position? News-cycle = ignore. Changed-position = act.

**Source:** Letters 1974, 1987, 2008, 2020

### The Coca-Cola Hold

**Rule:** Buffett bought $1.3B Coca-Cola 1988-1989; never sold a share. Cumulative dividends alone now exceed multiples of original cost. Holding through 1998's overpriced peak (where many would have sold), 2008-09, and 2020 was correct despite occasional fair-value premiums.

**Test:** Will the dividend stream alone return your cost basis within 30 years? If yes, premium-multiple selling is rarely correct on Inevitables.

**Source:** 1988 buy; never sold through 2025

### Sell When Prices Structurally Exceed Sense

**Rule:** Buffett wound up Buffett Partnership Ltd. in 1969 with a letter saying he could not find anything sensibly priced. When prices structurally exceed value across the opportunity set, raise cash and wait — even for years.

**Test:** Are bargains genuinely scarce, or are we just impatient? 1969 precedent says: yes, raise cash and wait — even if the index keeps going up for years.

**Source:** Buffett Partnership wind-down letter 1969

### Cash Is a Position to Hold, Not Defend

**Rule:** $325B+ in cash and Treasuries by year-end 2024 was a deliberate position, not a failure to deploy. Holding cash through 'career-risk' drawdowns is the precondition to deploying it at the next break.

**Test:** Are we holding cash because we don't see opportunities, or because we want to feel 'invested'? Discipline = OK to be in cash for years.

**Source:** Letter 2024; AGM 2024

### Don't Trim on First Sign of Pain

**Rule:** Pain alone is not signal. Most great holdings had multi-year drawdowns inside the position. Conversely, when the pain is structural not cyclical (Wells Fargo cross-sell scandal), exit decisively.

**Test:** Is the pain about the business or about the stock? If business, exit. If stock, ignore. Most quarterly pain is the latter.

**Source:** Wells Fargo arc; Letter 2020

### Don't Average Down on Compromised Trust

**Rule:** When trust is compromised at the top, it is not recoverable on any reasonable timeframe. The original fraud calls into question everything else management says.

**Test:** For each governance event, does it suggest 'isolated incident handled cleanly' or 'tip of an iceberg'? Iceberg-shaped = exit. The discount looks like a bargain but the disclosure is a sample, not the population.

**Source:** Wells Fargo eventual exit; Sokol 2011

### Refuse to Be Forced Into Action

**Rule:** Other people's deadlines are not your deadlines. Selling because you must show 'active management' is a fee-justification trap.

**Test:** If we did nothing this quarter, would the operator's 10-year IRR change? Usually no. If yes, ask why decision-velocity is wired to a calendar instead of a thesis.

**Source:** 1969, 2008, 2024 patience cycles

### Don't Overstay Past Genuine Decay

**Rule:** Buffett held Buffalo News and a network of small papers despite inevitable decline. Eventually divested 2020. Holding too long on a sentimentally important position is its own mistake.

**Test:** Is the candidate hanging on to a position out of identity rather than economics? Identity-anchored holdings systematically underperform thesis-anchored peers.

**Source:** Newspaper divestments 2018-2020

### The 2020 Airlines Sale — Update Priors When Facts Change

**Rule:** Buffett built airline positions 2016-2019, then exited entirely in April 2020 when COVID demonstrated structural fragility he had previously underweighted. Flexibility on a held position after public commitment is not weakness; it is discipline.

**Test:** Has new information genuinely changed the structural thesis? Then sell, even at a loss, even after public commitment, even if it embarrasses you.

**Source:** AGM May 2020 (airline exits)

### Plan for Succession Before You Need It

**Rule:** Berkshire's succession (Greg Abel as CEO from 2025) was planned for >15 years. Buffett's revealed bias: governance > urgency > brilliance. Smooth transitions need decade-long lead time.

**Test:** For any candidate operator over 65, is succession credible and named? 'We'll figure it out' = governance discount. The cost of unplanned succession is roughly one founder-era of compounding.

**Source:** AGM 2021 (Abel named); 2025 transition

### Insurance Pricing-Cycle Discipline

**Rule:** Insurance is the canonical example of 'stop writing when prices are too low.' Premium volume that requires below-historical pricing is loss-making in disguise — it just hasn't shown up yet.

**Test:** Is the insurer growing during a soft cycle? Often the best operators shrink and the worst ones grow, with the gap visible only 5-7 years later when the losses develop.

**Source:** Letter 1992; Letter 1999; Ajit Jain reinsurance discipline

### The 'If Stock Market Closed for 10 Years' Test

**Rule:** If the market closed today and re-opened in 10 years, would you still be content holding this? The framing eliminates the noise that drives most selling.

**Test:** Yes = hold. No = sell now while the market is open and the answer is still cheap.

**Source:** AGM repeatedly; Letter 1988

### Run a Long Game; Stop When the Game Stops

**Rule:** Capital allocators in their 60s and 70s tend to outperform young hot-shots — partly more reps, partly less to prove. The right exit time is when the framework breaks, not when the calendar says.

**Test:** Are decisions still being made by someone who treats their own capital as identical to outside capital? When that breaks, the operator is done — independent of age.

**Source:** Buffett 1965-2025; Munger 1978-2023

### Pareto / 90% From a Few Decisions

**Rule:** Of Berkshire's 60-year compounding, the bulk traces to a handful of decisions: GEICO, See's, Coca-Cola, Apple, BNSF, the float structure itself. Most of the rest is noise around those.

**Test:** When evaluating an allocator's record, ignore the average. Find the top 5 decisions and ask: were they conviction-bought-and-held, or accidents? Conviction is repeatable; accident is not.

**Source:** Berkshire 1965-2024 attribution

### Don't Revise the Framework Under Pressure

**Rule:** Healthy operators revise positions; unhealthy ones revise frameworks. When a position breaks, the discipline is to absorb the loss without breaking the rules that produced 99 prior wins.

**Test:** After a loss, is the operator changing position size, or changing the rule that allowed the position? Rule-changing under pressure is the structural cause of repeat blowups.

**Source:** Letter 1989 (Mistakes); Buffett response to 2008 financial crisis

## 2026 Overlay

#### Where the alpha is
- Japanese sogo shosha (Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo) — undervalued conglomerates with capital discipline, yen-funded; Berkshire raised stakes to ~9% across 2024
- Energy infrastructure with rate-base predictability (BHE, Occidental ~28%) — capital-intensive but regulated and decade-stable
- Domestic insurance franchises with structural cost advantages (GEICO efficiency turnaround, Ajit-led reinsurance discipline)
- Cash-generative US consumer brands with pricing power (Coca-Cola, American Express, Moody's) — held through every cycle
- Apple as a consumer-products business with switching costs — even after 2024 trims, the largest single equity position
- Sitting on $325B+ cash as positioning for the next forced-seller window, not as a forecast

#### Auto-reject
- Bitcoin / crypto / 'digital gold' speculation — produces nothing, requires a greater fool
- Cyclicals dressed as growth stories (airlines, auto OEMs, steel, shipping at peak)
- Tech businesses with unproven moats and venture-capital-cycle revenue
- Any business requiring a specific macro regime (rates, FX, geopolitics) to work
- Roll-up-by-acquisition stories without organic ROIC track record
- Heavily promoted SPACs and de-SPACed vehicles
- Insurance carriers growing during soft pricing cycles
- 'Toad-Princess' deals — premium price plus 'we'll fix it'
- Leveraged credit structures whose financing window is shorter than their business cycle

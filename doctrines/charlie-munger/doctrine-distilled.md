# The Munger Doctrine

> "Acquire worldly wisdom and adjust your behavior accordingly. If your new behavior gives you a little temporary unpopularity with your peer group... then to hell with them."

**About:** Distilled from a complete reading of *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (all 618 pages, eleven talks, the full 25-tendency Psychology of Human Misjudgment, and the complete investment framework). Three layers map to Munger's actual decision sequence: what to even look at (circle of competence + moat quality), how to analyze it (the lattice of multidisciplinary models, inversion, checklists), and when to act or sit still (the Too Hard pile, holding for decades, bias-triggered exit). The lattice is preserved in its proper home — Layer 2 — rather than flattened into priors.

---

## Layer 1 — Problem-Selection Taste
*What is worth working on at all*

The pre-filter is ruthless. Munger divides all investments into three baskets: YES / NO / TOO TOUGH TO UNDERSTAND. Most things go straight to Too Tough — not because they are objectively impossible to analyze, but because they fall outside the circle. Within the YES/maybe bucket, only four attributes survive: simple and understandable business, durable competitive moat, capable and trustworthy management, and a price that makes sense. The bar is set deliberately high: "wonderful business at fair price" beats "fair business at wonderful price."

### Circle of Competence
**Rule:** Only swing at pitches in your best cells. Reaching for the worst cells "would seriously reduce your chances of success." Define the boundary honestly — what you genuinely understand end-to-end — and stay inside it.

**Test:** Can you explain, in plain language, how this business makes money, what would destroy it, and why it has survived competition so far? If any answer requires deferring to an expert, the business is outside the circle.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Ted Williams / 77-Cell Strike Zone; USC Business School talk

### Simple and Understandable
**Rule:** Complex businesses are not merely harder to analyze — they are structurally more likely to conceal deterioration until it is too late. Simple businesses produce legible signals.

**Test:** Read the last five years of financials. Can you explain the revenue model, cost structure, and competitive dynamic in two sentences? If the answer requires a glossary, the business fails the test.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part III Investment Philosophy; The Three Baskets

### Durable Competitive Moat
**Rule:** Competitive advantage = a moat. "We tell our managers we want the moat widened every year. When we see a moat that's tenuous in any way — it's just too risky. We don't know how to evaluate that. And therefore we leave it alone."

**Test:** Name the moat explicitly — cost structure, switching costs, brand/Pavlovian conditioning, network effect, or regulatory position. Then ask: what has happened to the moat in the last five years? Widening or narrowing?

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — The Moat; Berkshire annual meetings

### Capable and Trustworthy Management
**Rule:** People of questionable character are an automatic disqualifier. Beyond character, look for owner-orientation — does management think like shareholders?

**Test:** Track capital allocation over five years. Did they buy back stock at high prices? Did they make dilutive acquisitions? Do their incentive structures reward long-term value creation or short-term earnings management?

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — The Investment Checklist; Psychology of Human Misjudgment — Incentives

### Wonderful Business at Fair Price
**Rule:** "A great business at a fair price is superior to a fair business at a great price." The core shift Munger drove in Buffett away from pure Graham statistical cheapness. A durable, compounding business bought at fair value outperforms a cheap, deteriorating one.

**Test:** Estimate total cash generated from now to eternity, discounted back. Then compare to today's price. Is there a margin of safety? Also: what is the opportunity cost vs. the next best use of capital?

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part III Investment Philosophy; The Foundation; Poor Charlie's Almanack — Investment Checklist

### Autocatalytic Business Model
**Rule:** The best businesses contain their own accelerant — Coca-Cola's Pavlovian conditioning + social proof + habit formation creates a self-reinforcing loop. Autocatalytic businesses compound without requiring additional capital injection.

**Test:** Does the business generate its own customers (virality, word-of-mouth, habit)? Does customer density make the product more valuable? Does brand/reputation reinforce itself? If the answer to all three is no, it is not autocatalytic.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Chemistry: Autocatalysis; Appendix: The Coca-Cola Demonstration

### Avoiding the Croupier Tax
**Rule:** "All the equity investors, in total, will surely bear a performance disadvantage per annum equal to the total croupiers' costs they have jointly elected to bear. This is an inescapable fact of life." High-friction structures (frequent trading, excessive fees, unnecessary intermediaries) destroy compounding at the foundation.

**Test:** Compute the all-in annual cost of the investment structure (fees, taxes from turnover, spread costs). Is the net-of-friction return plausibly above a low-cost index alternative? If not, the structure fails before the analysis even begins.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Economics: Croupier Costs; Febezzlement

### The Inevitability of Competitive Destruction
**Rule:** "Few businesses survive over multiple generations." Studying historical industry evolution reveals that almost no companies maintain competitive advantages intact. This is the base rate against which individual assessments must be made.

**Test:** Find three companies from the same industry twenty years ago. How many still exist in their original form with intact competitive advantages? Use the survivors as the denominator, not the examples.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Biology: Darwinian Natural Selection; History: The Inevitability of Competitive Destruction

## Layer 2 — Approach Taste
*Given the problem, which angle to attack from*

The lattice is the method. Once a business passes the pre-filter, Munger attacks it from all applicable disciplines simultaneously, not sequentially. The key tools are inversion (define what destroys the thesis, then work forward), checklists (the 25 psychological tendencies used as a structured override against intuition), and second-order thinking (supply/demand first-order effects are trivial; third-order effects are where insight lives). When multiple models converge on the same answer, the signal strength is immense — a Lollapalooza. When they conflict, the conflict itself is the insight.

### Inversion — Jacobi's Imperative
**Rule:** "It is not enough to think problems through forward. You must also think in reverse — many problems can't be solved forward." All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there.

**Test:** For any investment thesis, first construct the bear case in maximum-bad-faith: what would have to be true for this to be a disaster? Can each component of that bear case be disproved? If any cannot be disproved, the bear case is the forecast.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Mathematics: Inversion (Jacobi); Part VI Prescribed Practices

### Two-Track Analysis
**Rule:** Every important decision requires two simultaneous tracks: (1) what are the factors that really govern the interests involved, rationally considered? (2) what are the subconscious psychological influences operating on all parties? Ignoring either track produces systematic error.

**Test:** After completing the rational analysis, explicitly run the 25-tendency checklist. Which biases are active in you? In management? In the market? Does the rational conclusion survive the bias audit?

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Multiple Disciplines Combined: Two-Track Analysis

### Lollapalooza Effects — Convergent Models
**Rule:** "Especially big forces often come out of these one hundred models. When several models combine, you get lollapalooza effects." The signal is not additive — it is often exponential, like critical mass in nuclear physics.

**Test:** Count how many independent models (from different disciplines) converge on the same conclusion. One model = hypothesis. Three models = conviction. Five or more converging = near-certainty. Also check: are any models pointing in the opposite direction? A single strong counter-model defeats five weak confirming ones.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Multiple Disciplines: Lollapalooza Tendency (#25)

### The Incentive Override
**Rule:** "Perhaps the most important rule in management is 'Get the incentives right.'" Incentives shape cognition, not just behavior — people genuinely come to believe what their incentive structure rewards them for believing. This applies to management, analysts, auditors, and to yourself.

**Test:** For every key actor in the investment — CEO, CFO, auditor, sell-side analyst, fund manager recommending it — ask: what does their compensation structure reward? What would they have to believe for their paycheck to make sense? Now ask: is that what they are telling you?

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Psychology #1: Reward and Punishment Super-Response Tendency

### Second and Third-Order Effects
**Rule:** "The deeper insight: second and third-order effects on behavior from price changes are often more important than first-order effects." Medicare cost forecast was off by 1,000%+ because first-order thinking dominated policy analysis.

**Test:** After identifying the obvious first-order effect of any development, force yourself to trace second-order effects (how do others adapt?) and third-order effects (how does that adaptation change the original dynamic?). Write all three levels before forming a view.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Economics: Supply and Demand; Nine Defects of Academic Economics

### Scale Effects as Signal
**Rule:** "All large aggregations of capital eventually find it hell on earth to grow and thus find a lower rate of return." Scale effects are both the source of early moats and the eventual destroyer of returns. Understanding which phase a business occupies is central to the analysis.

**Test:** Plot revenue growth rate vs. time. Is the growth-rate slope declining? Compare incremental returns on capital over the last five years. A widening gap between total ROIC and incremental ROIC signals the scale trap.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Economics: Scale Effects; Berkshire annual meeting commentary

### Checklist Procedure for Biases
**Rule:** The 25 psychological tendencies are not trivia — they are a structured checklist to run against every important decision. "An idea or a fact is not worth more merely because it is easily available to you." The checklist forces you to audit what you are overweighting and underweighting.

**Test:** Before finalizing any investment decision, explicitly work through at minimum: Incentive-Caused Bias, Social Proof (is this consensus?), Availability Misweighing (am I over-indexing on vivid recent data?), Deprival-Super Reaction (am I holding because of sunk cost?), Inconsistency-Avoidance (am I defending a prior conclusion against new evidence?), and Overoptimism.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part II Psychology: The 25 Tendencies; Part VI Prescribed Practices

### Darwin's Anti-Confirmation Method
**Rule:** "Train yourself to intensively consider any evidence tending to disconfirm your hypotheses, especially your favorite hypotheses." Darwin made it a habit to write down any evidence tending to disconfirm his theory within thirty minutes, because he knew it would otherwise disappear.

**Test:** After forming an investment thesis, assign it to a dedicated devil's advocate role — either a different person or a deliberately scheduled second-pass. The question is not "what supports this?" but "what would make this wrong, and does that evidence exist?"

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Psychology #5: Inconsistency-Avoidance Tendency; Part IV Life Prescriptions: The Darwin Method

### Opportunity Cost as Primary Frame
**Rule:** "Intelligent people make decisions based on opportunity costs — in other words, it's your alternatives that matter. That's how we make all of our decisions." A good investment evaluated in isolation is often a poor investment relative to the next best use of capital.

**Test:** Before committing to any investment, explicitly state the next best alternative and its expected return. Is this investment meaningfully better, or are you rationalizing a desire to act?

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Economics: Opportunity Cost

## Layer 3 — Stopping Taste
*When to abandon, when to push, when "done enough"*

Munger's stopping rules are as important as his entry rules. The central discipline is the Too Hard pile — not a judgment that a business is bad, but a judgment that you cannot evaluate it with confidence. For positions already held: sit on cash when nothing qualifies, hold winners for decades (the tax math alone justifies it), and exit when Cialdini-trigger awareness flags a bias-driven rationalization for continuing to hold.

### The Too Hard Pile
**Rule:** All investments belong in one of three buckets: YES / NO / TOO TOUGH TO UNDERSTAND. "Many investor favorites (pharma, tech) go straight to 'too tough.'" Putting something in the Too Hard pile is not failure — it is discipline.

**Test:** After analysis, ask: can I explain why this business will still be dominant in ten years, in two sentences, with conviction? If the honest answer is "it depends on too many things I cannot model," it belongs in Too Hard, not in the portfolio.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part III Investment Philosophy: The Three Baskets; Circle of Competence

### Sit-On-Your-Ass Investing
**Rule:** "If you buy something because it's undervalued, then you have to think about selling it when it approaches your calculation of intrinsic value. That's hard. But if you can buy a few great companies, then you can sit on your ass. That's a good thing."

**Test:** Is the moat still intact and widening? Is management still owner-oriented? If yes to both, the presumption is hold. Any impulse to sell requires an affirmative case, not just fatigue or headline noise.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part III: Sit-On-Your-Ass Investing; The Tax Math

### The 20-Punch Card Discipline
**Rule:** "If you had a ticket with only 20 slots in it representing all the investments you could make in a lifetime, you would really think carefully about what you did and be forced to load up on what you'd really thought about." Scarcity forces quality.

**Test:** Before adding a new position, ask: is this among the twenty best investments I will ever make? If I had to give up an existing position to take it, which would I give up — and does that trade make sense? If not, do not add it.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part III: The 20-Punch Card

### Avoiding Envy and Resentment
**Rule:** "Envy is a really stupid sin because it's the only one you could never possibly have any fun at." Envy- and resentment-driven investment decisions — buying what competitors are buying, chasing returns to avoid appearing to lag — are structurally guaranteed to destroy compounding.

**Test:** Before any action, ask: am I doing this because the analysis supports it, or because someone else made money on it and I feel left out? Envy-driven buys should be placed immediately in the Too Hard pile.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Psychology #8: Envy/Jealousy Tendency; Part IV Life Prescriptions: What to Avoid

### Cialdini-Trigger Awareness as Exit Signal
**Rule:** When you catch yourself rationalizing a continued hold using social proof ("everyone else is still in"), reciprocation ("the management has been so good to me"), or authority ("the analyst says hold"), treat this as a bias-triggered defense of a position, not an analysis. Bias-triggered defenses are exit signals.

**Test:** Write down the three strongest reasons to hold. Now label each: is it a fact about the business, or is it a psychological tendency? If two of three are psychological, begin exiting.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part II Psychology: The 25 Tendencies; Robert Cialdini cited in Part VII Reading List

### Concentration Over Diversification
**Rule:** "The idea of excessive diversification is madness. In the United States, a person or institution with almost all wealth invested long-term, in just three fine domestic corporations, is securely rich." Diversification is a substitute for knowledge, not a supplement to it.

**Test:** Count positions. If more than ten, ask whether each additional position improves the expected outcome or merely reduces the discomfort of being wrong on any single one. Positions held for "diversification" rather than conviction belong in cash.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part III: On Diversification; The Concentration Corollary

### Avoiding Institutional Accounting Traps
**Rule:** "Treat financial reports and their underlying accounting with a Midwestern dose of skepticism. At best, they are merely the beginning of a proper calculation of intrinsic value." EBITDA: "Every time you see the word EBITDA, you should substitute the words 'bullshit earnings.'"

**Test:** Recalculate key metrics from scratch: true free cash flow (subtract all capex needed to maintain competitive position, not just maintenance capex), real working capital changes, true cost of options and pension obligations. If the recalculated numbers diverge significantly from reported numbers, reduce position or exit.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part III: Financial Reports; Contrarian Positions on Standard Finance

### Hold Winners for Decades — The Tax Math
**Rule:** Paying 35% tax annually on 15% returns yields approximately 9.75%/year compounded. Deferring the same tax to the end yields close to 15% compounded. "Plus: paying less to brokers, listening to less nonsense."

**Test:** Before selling a high-quality compounder, compute the tax drag of realizing gains vs. continuing to hold. In most cases, the hurdle rate for the replacement investment must be substantially higher to justify the friction of switching.

**Source:** Poor Charlie's Almanack — Part III: The Tax Math; Sit-On-Your-Ass Investing

## 2026 Overlay

#### Where the alpha is
- Durable simple businesses with identifiable moats — consumer brands with Pavlovian conditioning, infrastructure with genuine switching costs, financial businesses with pricing power
- Low-cost index-like quality compounders held for decades, exploiting the tax-deferral math
- Businesses where the autocatalytic loop is still early — brand density, habit formation, and network effects still widening the moat
- Family-controlled businesses where management incentives are aligned with owners across multiple generations

#### Auto-reject
- Crypto and speculative digital assets — too tough, no durable competitive moat, incentive structures misaligned
- Complex derivatives and structured products — "The system is almost insanely irresponsible... accounting for derivatives in America is a sewer"
- Levered ETFs and leveraged speculation — interrupts compounding at its foundation
- Fashionable technology businesses dependent on a single product cycle with no moat evidence
- Any investment requiring Black-Scholes for long-dated options or precision Newtonian formulas for intrinsic value — Physics Envy masquerading as analysis
- Things I don't understand: if I cannot explain it simply, it goes in Too Hard, not in the portfolio

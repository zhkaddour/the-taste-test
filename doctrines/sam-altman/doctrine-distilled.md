# The Altman Doctrine

> "The most important things are to work on the right problem, find the right team, and don't run out of money."

**About:** A three-layer filter for building transformative companies. Distilled from 'How to be Successful' (samaltman.com, 2019), the 'Startup Playbook' (YC), 'What I Wish Someone Had Told Me' (2023), 'The Intelligence Age' (2024), the Stanford CS183B lectures (2014), Lex Fridman #367 and #419, the Acquired podcast interview (2023), and the full OpenAI blog corpus (2019-2026).

---
## Layer 1 — Problem-Selection Taste
*What is worth working on at all*

Altman's problem-selection doctrine is organized around one principle: civilizational stakes. Problems worth working on should matter at the scale of humanity, not just a market segment. The filter combines importance with timing — the right problem at the wrong moment is as bad as the wrong problem. The biggest breakthroughs often seem unambitious at the outset, but their potential is visible to those with the right frame.

### Work on What Matters at Civilization Scale
**Rule:** The most important work addresses problems that, if solved, materially improve the condition of humanity. Every founder should answer: 'If this succeeds, what changes about the world?'

**Test:** Can the founder state in one sentence what changes about the world if this succeeds — at civilizational scale, not market-segment scale? A specific improvement for a narrow customer segment is not a passing answer.

**Source:** How to be Successful, 2019; Lex Fridman #367

### The Market Timing Imperative
**Rule:** Most failed startups had the right idea at the wrong time. The best startups ride a wave that is just beginning — where the enabling conditions have just arrived.

**Test:** Name the specific enabling condition (technology, regulation, behavior change) that has arrived in the last 3 years that makes this startup possible now but not 5 years ago. If the founder cannot name it, timing is not understood.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B, 2014

### Founder-Market Fit Over Product-Market Fit
**Rule:** Product-market fit can be found by many founders; founder-market fit determines whether you can outlast the competition to find it.

**Test:** Does the founder have an unfair insight from direct domain experience — not just research, but lived exposure? Can they articulate something specific they know about the problem that took years to learn?

**Source:** How to be Successful; Stanford CS183B

### Hard Problems Attract Better People
**Rule:** Ambitious, hard problems attract the best talent. If the mission is too modest, you'll struggle to hire the extraordinary people you need.

**Test:** Would a world-class engineer or researcher turn down a better-paying offer to work on this mission? If the mission can't create that pull, its ambition level is insufficient.

**Source:** How to be Successful; Startup Playbook

### The Adjacency to Transformative Technology
**Rule:** The most powerful startups are often those building applications that only become possible once a transformative technology arrives. Identify what's just arrived.

**Test:** Name the specific transformative technology that just unlocked this startup. Is this startup one of the first 5-10 to exploit that unlocking? If the wave is already crowded, the adjacency timing has passed.

**Source:** The Intelligence Age, 2024; OpenAI blog

### Non-Consensus and Right
**Rule:** Consensus opportunities are oversubscribed by definition. The only opportunities with exceptional upside are those where you believe something true that most smart people don't yet believe.

**Test:** State the non-consensus claim in one sentence. Then poll three smart domain insiders. If all three agree, it's consensus — not a non-consensus bet.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Acquired podcast, 2023

### Favor Markets That Will Grow
**Rule:** Assess the market you want to exist in 10 years, not the market that exists today. The correct TAM analysis is forward-looking.

**Test:** What is the 10-year market size projection and what specific trend drives it? If the projection requires no change in the current environment, it's a present-market bet, not a future-market bet.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### The Problem Should Be a Hair-on-Fire Problem
**Rule:** The best startup problems are ones where users are actively suffering. Aspirins sell themselves; vitamins require convincing.

**Test:** Can the founder name 3 users who are actively suffering from this problem today — people who would pay immediately for a partial solution? If users are mildly inconvenienced rather than actively suffering, it's a vitamin.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### Beware the Idea You Can't Give Away
**Rule:** If 100 smart people find the idea interesting but nobody urgently wants it, you have an interesting idea, not a business. The test is behavior change, not approval.

**Test:** Have any users changed their behavior — signed up, paid, cancelled another service — based on this product? Interest without behavior change is the vitamin signal.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### The Compounding Startup
**Rule:** Startup ideas that compound — where success at step 1 creates the platform for step 2 — produce disproportionate outcomes. Name the first three stages explicitly.

**Test:** Can the founder name stages 1, 2, and 3 of their compounding plan, and explain why stage 1 success uniquely enables stage 2? Missing specificity on the compounding logic = the compounding is wishful.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; How to be Successful

### The AGI Frame for Problem Selection
**Rule:** AI will compress the timeline on nearly every scientific and technological problem. Every founder should ask: which problems does AI unlock in my domain?

**Test:** Name the specific bottleneck in this domain that AI eliminates or compresses. If the answer is 'AI makes everything easier,' the frame is too generic — identify the specific gate AI breaks.

**Source:** The Intelligence Age, 2024; OpenAI blog

### Neglected and Important
**Rule:** The best problems are both important and neglected. Important without neglect = a hundred competitors. Neglected without importance = no market.

**Test:** Count the well-funded startups working on this exact problem today. If >5 are Series A or later, the problem is not neglected. If zero are, ask why — is it unimportant, or genuinely overlooked?

**Source:** How to be Successful; Startup Playbook

### Startup Problems Should Feel Slightly Crazy
**Rule:** If your idea sounds completely reasonable to everyone, it probably isn't ambitious enough to be a great startup.

**Test:** Describe the startup to a smart, skeptical professional outside the industry. Does she nod politely, or does she push back with "that seems really hard / premature / crazy"? Polite nod = insufficient ambition.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Acquired, 2023

### Early Revenue Is a Filter, Not a Goal
**Rule:** Finding users who will pay immediately is a signal that you've found a real problem. Revenue at the earliest stage is primarily feedback.

**Test:** Have any users paid for this at any price, even a token amount? Payment is the strongest signal that the problem is real. Zero paying users at prototype stage = unvalidated problem.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### Starting Too Small Is Usually Right
**Rule:** Starting narrow and dominating before expanding is almost always better than addressing a broad market from day one.

**Test:** Can the founder name the single customer archetype they are winning first — with enough specificity that a salesperson could cold-call a list? A broad initial market signals a failure to identify the beachhead.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### The Problem That Will Exist at Scale
**Rule:** Some problems only become visible at scale. The best companies anticipate these problems and build for the scaled world before it arrives.

**Test:** At 10x current scale, does this problem become more severe, not less? If the problem dissolves at scale, this is an early-stage solution that loses its market as it grows.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### Work on Problems Where You Have Unfair Insight
**Rule:** The best startup ideas come from direct experience with the problem. Unfair insight is the most sustainable competitive advantage.

**Test:** What is the specific insight the founder has that competitors don't? It should require years of direct exposure to acquire — not reading, not research, but experience.

**Source:** How to be Successful; Startup Playbook

### The 10-Year Test
**Rule:** Would this problem still be worth solving in 10 years even if technology changed dramatically? If yes, you're working on an enduring human need.

**Test:** Describe a plausible technology shift in 5 years and ask: does this problem still exist and does this company still matter? If a single technology shift dissolves the market, the need is not enduring.

**Source:** How to be Successful

### Avoid Solutions Looking for Problems
**Rule:** Start with the problem, not the technology. If your pitch starts with 'we built X, now looking for applications,' the approach is backward.

**Test:** Ask the founder to describe the problem before describing the solution. If the first sentence mentions the technology, the approach is inverted. The problem must be articulable without referencing the solution.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### The Defensibility Horizon
**Rule:** Every startup needs a specific answer to 'why won't a big company copy this in 18 months?' — network effects, switching costs, proprietary tech, regulatory moat, or an unreplicable team.

**Test:** Name the specific moat and the mechanism by which it prevents a well-resourced incumbent from copying in 18 months. 'We'll move faster' is not a moat. Require a structural answer.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

## Layer 2 — Approach Taste
*Given the problem, which angle to attack from*

Altman's approach doctrine is organized around speed, conviction, and talent density. The core thesis: the biggest advantage a startup has over an incumbent is speed — the ability to make and execute decisions in days that would take a large company months. Every approach doctrine is in service of maintaining that speed without sacrificing quality.

### Speed Is a Moat
**Rule:** Every week of faster iteration compounds. Speed is not just an operating preference — it is a competitive strategy. The team that ships faster learns faster.

**Test:** What is the average time from decision to shipped code this quarter? Compare against the founding team's subjective sense of "fast." If the answer requires minutes to compute, the team has lost the habit of measuring speed.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook

### Make Something People Want, Above All Else
**Rule:** The primary job of every startup is to make something people actually use, recommend, and would miss if it disappeared.

**Test:** Would current users be meaningfully upset if the product disappeared tomorrow? Survey a sample: "How disappointed would you be if you could no longer use this?" >40% "very disappointed" is the PMF threshold.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; YC core doctrine

### Growth Is the Primary Feedback Loop
**Rule:** Consistent week-over-week growth is the single most important signal. 5-7% WoW sustained for a year indicates product-market fit has been found.

**Test:** What is the trailing 12-week organic WoW growth rate? Below 5%: pre-PMF, change something. 5-7%: PMF signal, accelerate. Below 0%: crisis.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### Do the Unscalable Thing First
**Rule:** In the early stage, do things that don't scale: personally recruit every user, manually perform operations that will later be automated.

**Test:** What is one thing the team is doing manually today that won't scale past 10x? If the answer is "nothing — we built it to scale from day one," early-stage learning is being skipped.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### Talk to Users Constantly
**Rule:** Direct, unmediated founder conversation with users is the highest-value activity for most founders in the early stage. Not surveys — founders talking directly to users.

**Test:** How many unmediated founder-to-user conversations happened this week? Fewer than 3 in an early-stage startup is too few. "The team handles it" is a red flag at seed.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### User Obsession Over Competitor Obsession
**Rule:** Competitor obsession leads to feature parity races. User obsession leads to genuine breakthroughs. If your roadmap is driven by competitor analysis, you're reacting.

**Test:** Of the last 5 roadmap decisions, how many were driven by user interviews and how many by competitor moves? More than 2 competitor-driven decisions in 5 = reactive posture.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### Hire People Better Than You at Every Role
**Rule:** Exceptional people are not incrementally better than good people — they are 10x better. They elevate everyone around them.

**Test:** For each key hire, can the founder name a specific reason this person is better than the founder would be in that role? "We needed to fill the position" is not a reason.

**Source:** How to be Successful; What I Wish, 2023

### Recruit Missionaries, Not Mercenaries
**Rule:** Missionaries optimize for the mission. They are more resilient, more creative in constrained environments, more trustworthy with hard problems.

**Test:** Why did each of the first 10 hires join? If more than 3 cite compensation as the primary reason, the team skews mercenary. Missionaries cite the mission first.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### Fire Fast, Carefully
**Rule:** Keeping underperformers too long is one of the most common and costly mistakes. The right time to fire is usually 3-6 months earlier than most founders actually do it.

**Test:** Is there anyone on the team today who the founder would not hire again knowing what they now know? If yes and the person is still employed >90 days into that realization, firing is overdue.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023

### Give Equity to the People Who Deserve It
**Rule:** Equity is the primary alignment mechanism. People who hold meaningful equity think like owners; those who hold little think like employees.

**Test:** Does every key contributor hold enough equity that a good exit would be genuinely life-changing for them? If the answer is no for any key person, alignment is broken.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### The Right Investors Change the Trajectory
**Rule:** The best investors bring more than capital — access to talent, honest advice, pattern recognition. The wrong investors bring money and noise.

**Test:** Name two specific non-capital contributions the lead investor has made in the last 6 months. If the answer is only "they made intros," investigate whether the intros converted to anything.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### Clear Communication of Mission and Metrics
**Rule:** Every person should state the mission in one sentence and the three most important metrics the company is tracking this quarter.

**Test:** Ask three random employees to state the mission and the top three metrics unprompted. If answers diverge, communication has failed. Misaligned answers on metrics are a management failure.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook

### The Self-Belief Prerequisite
**Rule:** Founders who need external validation to maintain conviction will fold when things get hard. Conviction must be internally sourced.

**Test:** Describe the company's lowest moment in the last 12 months. Was the founder's conviction maintained from internal reasoning or rebuilt after positive external feedback? Internally sourced is the right answer.

**Source:** How to be Successful; What I Wish, 2023

### Move Fast and Have High Standards Simultaneously
**Rule:** Speed and quality are not necessarily in conflict. The best teams ship fast AND maintain high standards — because they've internalized what matters.

**Test:** In the last quarter, can the team name something they shipped fast AND something they refused to ship because the quality was insufficient? Both signals must be present.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023

### The Priority Stack
**Rule:** Every week, the company should have one priority above all others. Companies that have 10 priorities have zero priorities.

**Test:** Ask the founder to name the single top company priority this week without hesitation. Hesitation or a list of priorities is the failure signal.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook

### Long-Term Thinking in Short-Term Actions
**Rule:** Think in decades; act in weeks. Every short-term action should be the week's best expression of a long-term strategy.

**Test:** For this week's top priority, can the founder trace the direct line from this action to the 10-year strategy? If the action is purely reactive or tactical with no long-term logic, the discipline is missing.

**Source:** How to be Successful; various Altman interviews

### Fundraise Efficiently, Then Get Back to Work
**Rule:** Fundraising is a necessary interruption, not the job. The best founders raise from the first investors who offer strong terms, then return to product work.

**Test:** How many weeks did the last fundraise take? More than 8 weeks for a seed round, or more than 12 for a Series A, suggests fundraising is consuming disproportionate founder attention.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; What I Wish, 2023

### The Board Is a Tool, Not a Boss
**Rule:** The best founders respect their boards, keep them informed, and use them as sounding boards — but make decisions based on their own judgment and user data.

**Test:** Name the last board meeting where the founder disagreed with a board suggestion and proceeded with their own plan. If this has never happened, the founder may be over-deferring.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Acquired, 2023

### Manage Your Energy, Not Your Schedule
**Rule:** The founder's most important resource is cognitive energy. Protect large blocks of time for the highest-leverage thinking.

**Test:** How many uninterrupted 2+ hour blocks per week does the founder have for deep work? Fewer than 3 suggests the schedule is managed rather than energy.

**Source:** How to be Successful; What I Wish, 2023

### Think in Exponentials, Not Linears
**Rule:** Most people think in linear extrapolation. Train yourself to ask: what does this look like if the growth rate holds for 10 years?

**Test:** Project the current WoW growth rate out 3 years. Does the founder's planning reflect that outcome? If the 3-year plan feels wildly optimistic compared to current trajectory, the plan and the thesis are misaligned.

**Source:** The Intelligence Age, 2024; Moore's Law for Everything, 2021

### Own Your Mistakes
**Rule:** Teams that say 'we were wrong, here's what we're doing differently' recover faster and earn more trust than teams that explain why external factors were primarily responsible.

**Test:** Name the last significant mistake and the internal cause identified. If the explanation is primarily external ("the market shifted, the investor wasn't ready"), ownership is lacking.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023

### Management by Context, Not Control
**Rule:** Provide context — why decisions are being made, what the priorities are — rather than control. Context-rich teams make better decisions locally.

**Test:** Ask a mid-level employee why the top company priority exists. If they can trace the logic from mission to strategy to priority, management by context is working. If they just know the priority but not the why, context is thin.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook

### Network as Strategic Asset
**Rule:** The person you hire in 3 years should be someone you've been cultivating a relationship with for 2 years. Build networks before you need them.

**Test:** Name 3 people the company will need in 18 months and confirm the founder has an existing relationship with each. If all three are strangers, network investment is behind plan.

**Source:** How to be Successful; Stanford CS183B

### Compound Personal Credibility
**Rule:** Every accurate prediction, every honest acknowledgment of failure, every promise kept adds to the credibility stock. Manage it actively.

**Test:** Has the founder ever publicly acknowledged being wrong about a meaningful prediction or decision in the last 12 months? If never, either the founder is unusually right or not managing credibility honestly.

**Source:** How to be Successful; What I Wish, 2023

### Frugality as a Feature
**Rule:** The discipline of operating frugally produces better decisions. Startups that raise too much early make expensive, reversible mistakes without the learning that scarcity forces.

**Test:** What is the current monthly burn and runway? More than 24 months runway at seed should trigger a question about whether spending is appropriately constrained.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### The Weekly All-Hands as Truth Machine
**Rule:** Share both good and bad news transparently. Teams can handle the truth; they can't function well without it.

**Test:** In the last 3 all-hands, was any significant bad news shared? If all-hands are exclusively positive-news events, the truth machine has been replaced by a morale machine.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023; OpenAI culture

### The AI Transformation of Software Development
**Rule:** A 5-person team with the right AI tools can build what previously required 50. Ambition should scale up accordingly.

**Test:** Is the team's AI tooling investment proportional to the productivity multiplier available? If a competitor could achieve the same output with 5 fewer engineers using available tools, the team's ambition is too low.

**Source:** The Intelligence Age, 2024

## Layer 3 — Stopping Taste
*When to abandon, when to push, when "done enough"*

Altman's stopping doctrine distinguishes between stopping signals that come from outside (bad press, investor skepticism, competitive pressure) and stopping signals that come from inside (users don't actually want this, growth has stopped for unexplained reasons, the team has lost conviction). The former are often noise; the latter are almost always signal.

### Growth Is the Primary Stay Signal
**Rule:** If a startup is growing consistently — 5-7%+ WoW in the early stage — almost every other problem is solvable. Lack of growth is the primary stop signal.

**Test:** What is the trailing 8-week WoW organic growth rate? Below 3% for 8+ consecutive weeks without a clear diagnosis and a concrete change in progress is the primary stop signal.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### The 18-Month Signal
**Rule:** If a startup hasn't found meaningful traction in 18 months despite genuine attempts with different approaches, consider whether the problem or the founding team is the constraint.

**Test:** Has the company had genuine traction (5%+ WoW sustained for >8 weeks) at any point in the last 18 months? If no, force the problem/team diagnosis explicitly.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### Pivot the Product, Not the Mission
**Rule:** Founders who pivot the mission rarely recover. Pivoting the product — finding a different solution to the same problem — is healthy and often necessary.

**Test:** When a pivot is considered, can the founder articulate how the new product still solves the same core user problem? If the problem changes, it is a mission pivot, not a product pivot.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### Talent Density as the Stopping Test
**Rule:** Ask periodically: would you hire every person on your team today? If the 'no' list exceeds 20%, you have a significant talent density problem.

**Test:** Have every founder independently identify team members they would not re-hire today. If the lists overlap for >20% of the team, talent density has fallen below the critical threshold.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023

### Kill Features Without Mercy
**Rule:** Features that don't serve the core value proposition dilute focus and slow the team. Kill them with the same energy you'd bring to a key hire decision.

**Test:** What features exist in the product today that fewer than 20% of active users touch? Each is a candidate for killing. If the list is long and no features have been killed in 6 months, the pruning reflex is broken.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### Ignore Most Outside Noise
**Rule:** Press, Twitter, competitor announcements — almost none of this is useful signal. The signal that matters is user behavior.

**Test:** In the last month, how many strategic decisions were influenced by press coverage or competitor moves versus user behavior data? If the ratio favors external noise, the decision filter is broken.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023; Startup Playbook

### When to Stop Fundraising
**Rule:** Stop as soon as you have enough runway to reach your next important milestone. More money than needed is a distraction.

**Test:** Define the next concrete milestone and the capital required to reach it. Is current runway ≥ that amount plus a 25% buffer? If yes, stop fundraising now.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### The Mission Test for Staying
**Rule:** Founders should stay as long as they are the best people to execute the mission and remain genuinely committed to it. The right reason to leave is that someone else would execute better.

**Test:** Would the founder start this company again today knowing what they know? If the honest answer is no, staying is a sunk-cost decision.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### The Great vs. Good Company Threshold
**Rule:** At every stage: are we on the great path or the good path? If good, what would need to change to get to great?

**Test:** At current trajectory, does this company reach a $1B+ outcome in 10 years? If the honest answer is "a solid acquisition for $50-100M," name the specific change required for the great path, or accept that the company is on the good path.

**Source:** How to be Successful; Stanford CS183B

### Stop When the Founders Have Lost Conviction
**Rule:** When founders have genuinely lost conviction in the mission, the company is effectively over regardless of runway or growth metrics.

**Test:** Ask the founder directly: "If you had enough personal money and didn't need the exit, would you still build this?" If the answer is hesitant or no, conviction has been lost.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Acquired, 2023

### The Acqui-hire vs. Real Exit Test
**Rule:** When considering an exit, ask honestly: is this a real acquisition or a team rescue? The latter is fine, but be honest about what value has actually been created.

**Test:** Does the acquisition offer value the product, technology, or customer relationships — or primarily the team? If the acquirer would be equally happy taking only the team, this is an acqui-hire.

**Source:** Startup Playbook

### When to Stop Scaling
**Rule:** Scaling before product-market fit is the most common cause of expensive startup failure. The signals: growth requires active selling, churn is high, users aren't referring others unprompted.

**Test:** Three checks before scaling spend: (1) Is 5%+ WoW organic growth sustained? (2) Is monthly churn below 3%? (3) Are >20% of new users coming from referrals? If any is no, do not scale.

**Source:** Startup Playbook; Stanford CS183B

### The 'Would You Start This Today?' Test
**Rule:** Periodically ask: knowing everything you know now, would you start this company today? Apply this question every six months.

**Test:** Ask this question cold, before reviewing any metrics. The first honest emotional reaction is the signal. Rationalization follows quickly — capture the pre-rationalization answer.

**Source:** Various Altman interviews; Startup Playbook

### Stop Protecting Failing Bets
**Rule:** The willingness to acknowledge a failing bet and reallocate is one of the most valuable executive skills. It requires public acknowledgment of error.

**Test:** In the last 12 months, has any significant bet been publicly acknowledged as failed and resources reallocated? If every bet is still alive, either judgment is unusually good or failing bets are being protected.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023; OpenAI operating history

### The Values Test for Staying
**Rule:** When continuing requires systematic dishonesty to investors, employees, or users about what is working and what isn't, stop. The damage to personal credibility outweighs the potential upside.

**Test:** Is the company's current fundraising narrative consistent with what the founder tells their closest advisors? Any gap between the fundraising story and the honest internal story is the values test failure.

**Source:** What I Wish, 2023

## 2026 Overlay

### Where the alpha is
- AI-native applications: products fundamentally restructured around AI capabilities, not just AI-augmented versions of old products
- Energy infrastructure: AI's energy demands make compute-adjacent energy companies among the most important businesses of the next decade
- Universal basic compute: access to AI tools as infrastructure — the policy and business implications
- Healthcare and drug discovery: AI-enabled acceleration of the longest-cycle industry now shows near-term results
- Physical AI: robotics, autonomous systems, and AI that operates in the physical world

### Auto-reject
- AI wrappers with no proprietary data, model, or workflow moat — will be commoditized
- Consumer social products without a clear network effect defense against platform algorithm changes
- B2B SaaS in categories where AI is replacing the workflow the software was designed to support
- Business models that depend on regulatory arbitrage as the primary moat
- Any startup that cannot answer 'why now?' with a specific technology or market change from the last 3 years

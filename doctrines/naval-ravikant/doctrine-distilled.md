# The Naval Doctrine

> "Seek wealth, not money or status. Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep."

**About:** A three-layer filter for building wealth and choosing work. Distilled from the 'How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)' tweetstorm (May 2018), 'The Almanack of Naval Ravikant' (Jorgenson, 2020), the Naval podcast corpus, Tim Ferriss interviews (#97, #250, #473), Joe Rogan #1309, and the full @naval Twitter corpus (2007–present).

---
## Layer 1 — Problem-Selection Taste
*What is worth working on at all*

Naval's entire framework begins with one distinction: wealth versus money versus status. Most people confuse these three and optimize for the wrong one. Problem-selection means choosing work where you can build equity — ownership of something that generates value while you sleep — rather than renting out your time for a salary. The filter is applied before any tactics: wrong category of work = no amount of skill saves you.

### Wealth vs. Money vs. Status
**Rule:** Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep; money is transfer; status is social rank. Most people play money or status games and wonder why they never build wealth.

**Test:** Does this work produce assets (equity, IP, audience, brand) that generate value without the person's direct participation — or does it produce income that stops when the person stops? Income without asset creation is the money game, not the wealth game.

**Source:** How to Get Rich tweetstorm, May 2018

### Specific Knowledge First
**Rule:** Specific knowledge is knowledge you cannot be trained for. It lives at the intersection of genuine curiosity, natural obsession, and things you learned before you knew you were learning.

**Test:** Can this knowledge be acquired in a structured training program or bootcamp? If yes, it is not specific knowledge — it is a trainable skill that will be competed away. Specific knowledge feels like play to the person who has it.

**Source:** How to Get Rich; Almanack

### Genuine Curiosity Beats Forced Study
**Rule:** Curiosity is the most powerful learning accelerant. Specific knowledge must feel like play to you even if it looks like work to others.

**Test:** Does the person voluntarily spend time on this domain without external obligation — reading, experimenting, discussing — outside of work hours? If the interest requires external motivation, it is not genuine curiosity.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Avoid Renting Out Your Time
**Rule:** Any work where inputs and outputs are directly coupled is a treadmill with no exit. Wealth requires decoupling time from output.

**Test:** If the person stops working for 6 months, does income stop immediately? If yes, time and output are coupled and no wealth accumulation is occurring. The test is whether income continues without direct participation.

**Source:** How to Get Rich; Almanack

### Productize Yourself
**Rule:** What is the most scalable expression of you? Code, books, podcasts, courses — permissionless leverage that scales your specific knowledge to millions.

**Test:** Can the person's current specific knowledge be expressed in a form that serves 1,000 people as well as it serves 1, without proportionally more time? If not, productization has not been achieved.

**Source:** How to Get Rich; Almanack; Tim Ferriss #473

### The Four Types of Leverage
**Rule:** Labor, capital, code, media. Code and media are the new leverage — permissionless, free to replicate, available to anyone with a laptop.

**Test:** Which leverage types is the person currently deploying? Labor and capital require others' permission. Code and media do not. What % of current income comes from permissionless leverage?

**Source:** How to Get Rich; Almanack

### Code and Media Are Permissionless
**Rule:** Capital and labor require someone's permission. Code and media do not. Permissionless leverage is available to the intellectual, not only the connected.

**Test:** Does the current work require someone else's approval to scale it — a manager, investor, or institution? If yes, leverage is not permissionless. Code deployed and media published require no ongoing permission to distribute.

**Source:** How to Get Rich; Almanack

### Work With People Who Have All Three
**Rule:** High intelligence, high energy, high integrity. All three are required. Any missing property makes the combination dangerous or useless.

**Test:** For each key collaborator: score intelligence, energy, and integrity independently. Is any score below 7/10? One low score disqualifies the combination — a smart, energetic person with low integrity is dangerous.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### Long-Term Games With Long-Term People
**Rule:** Trust compounds. Reputation takes years to build and seconds to destroy. The best deals come to people who are trusted unconditionally.

**Test:** Has the person maintained 3+ long-term professional relationships (5+ years) built on repeated demonstrated integrity? If the relationship graph is mostly short-term or transactional, long-term trust has not been built.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### The Judgment Leverage
**Rule:** With infinite leverage, judgment becomes the binding constraint. Cultivate and sell your judgment — the higher the leverage, the more valuable a good decision.

**Test:** Is the person currently in a role where their judgment is the binding constraint — where a wrong decision destroys enormous value and a right one creates it? If they are executing others' judgments, leverage is blocked by the org structure.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Societal Leverage via Technology
**Rule:** Every software product, every published idea that gets adopted is society deploying your work without your involvement.

**Test:** Is the person's work currently being used by people they've never met, without their active involvement? If all value delivery requires personal presence, societal leverage has not been achieved.

**Source:** Naval podcast; Almanack; Tim Ferriss #97

### The Best Business = High Margins + Scalable
**Rule:** Software businesses are the canonical example. Low margins that scale with revenue are structurally incapable of building wealth.

**Test:** What is the gross margin on this business and does it improve, stay flat, or decline as scale increases? Gross margins that decline with scale (service businesses) are structurally bad for wealth creation.

**Source:** Naval podcast

### Work for Equity, Not Salary
**Rule:** Salary is renting your time. Equity is ownership of a machine that runs without you.

**Test:** What % of current compensation is equity? Is that equity in a machine that runs without the person's participation? Equity in a professional services firm is mostly salary in disguise.

**Source:** How to Get Rich; Almanack

### Accountability Unlocks Leverage
**Rule:** Taking ownership under your own name is what unlocks the ability to accumulate wealth and reputation.

**Test:** Is the person doing their most important work under their own name and taking public accountability for its outcomes? Anonymous work or work under an institution's brand does not build personal leverage.

**Source:** How to Get Rich; Almanack

### Find the Intersection of Three
**Rule:** The ideal work sits where: what you're good at, what you enjoy, and what the world pays for all overlap. Any two of three is insufficient.

**Test:** Score the current work on all three dimensions: (1) is this person among the top 10% in their domain? (2) do they lose track of time doing it? (3) does the market pay well for it? All three must score ≥7/10.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Avoid Zero-Sum Status Games
**Rule:** Status games are zero-sum. Wealth games are positive-sum. People playing status games try to tear others down.

**Test:** Does success in this work require someone else to fail, or does it create new value? Status game signals: comparing ranks, competitive credentialing, positional thinking. Wealth game signals: creating new products and audiences.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### Build in the New World, Not the Old
**Rule:** The old world required capital, connections, credentials, geography. The new world requires internet access and specific knowledge.

**Test:** Does this business model depend on old-world advantages (location, institutional access, traditional credentialing) that the internet has made less important? If yes, the model is competing on a declining advantage.

**Source:** Naval podcast

### The Retirement Frame
**Rule:** Retirement is not stopping work. It is reaching a state where you do what you want, when you want, with whom you want.

**Test:** Is the person's current work moving toward a state where they have full time, location, and collaborator autonomy — or is it building a golden cage? The retirement test is freedom, not financial number.

**Source:** Almanack; Farnam Street

### Pick the Game You Want to Play, Then Learn the Rules
**Rule:** Most people play games they fell into. Explicitly choose the game. The right game, played with specific knowledge and leverage, compounds for decades.

**Test:** Can the person explicitly articulate which game they are playing (wealth, status, or money), why they chose it, and when they consciously made that choice? Defaulting into a game is not playing it.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### The Internet Enables Global-Scale Niches
**Rule:** Before the internet, being the world's best at something very specific was economically worthless. Now extreme specificity is a business model.

**Test:** Is the specific knowledge narrow enough that it would have been economically worthless before the internet, but now has a global audience? The narrower and more specific, the more defensible.

**Source:** Naval podcast; @naval Twitter

### Don't Partner With Cynics or Pessimists
**Rule:** Their beliefs become self-fulfilling. Pessimists systematically underinvest in building.

**Test:** Does this potential partner regularly frame situations as losing propositions or attribute failure to external causes? Persistent negativity is self-fulfilling and contagious.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### The Darwinian Filter of Starting a Company
**Rule:** Starting a company is the most direct test of specific knowledge, leverage, accountability, and judgment.

**Test:** Has the person attempted to build something under their own name — a company, a substantial project, a product — and survived the market's feedback? The company-founding filter reveals what other tests cannot.

**Source:** Almanack

### Signals of Specific Knowledge
**Rule:** Three signals: you teach yourself better than any class could, people ask your opinion unsolicited, you lose track of time doing it.

**Test:** Run all three checks: (1) has this person self-taught beyond what any formal program could teach? (2) do people actively seek their opinion in this domain? (3) does time pass unnoticed when working on it? All three must be true.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Build Distribution Early
**Rule:** Distribution is leverage. An audience is an asset that amplifies every future product you make.

**Test:** Does the person have an audience — email list, social following, professional reputation — that would distribute a new product on day one without paid marketing? No audience = starting from zero on each new product.

**Source:** Naval podcast; AngelList

### The Compounding Horizon Must Be Long
**Rule:** Compounding works best over decades, not years. Choose a domain you can compound in for 20 years.

**Test:** Is this domain one where the person can realistically compound for 20 years and still be at the frontier? Domains that evolve too fast reset the compound clock; domains that don't evolve produce diminishing returns.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### Learn to Sell and Learn to Build
**Rule:** If you can do both, you will be unstoppable. These are the only two skills that matter for a founder.

**Test:** Can the person both build (create the product) and sell (acquire customers)? For a founding team: is one person strong on each? A team of only builders or only sellers is incomplete.

**Source:** tweetstorm; Almanack

### Ignore Salary-Capped Paths Early
**Rule:** Any career with a hard salary ceiling is structurally limited. The best early careers are fat-tailed.

**Test:** What is the maximum lifetime income from this path assuming extraordinary performance? If the answer has a ceiling below $10M lifetime, the path is salary-shaped, not wealth-shaped.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### The Specific Knowledge Stack
**Rule:** Specific knowledge can be stacked. The combination of two rare skills creates a unique position nobody else occupies.

**Test:** Name two rare skills the person has. At the intersection, how many other people globally have both? If the answer is "hundreds," the stack is not yet unique. If "dozens," the position is defensible.

**Source:** Almanack; Tim Ferriss #250

### The Path of No Regrets
**Rule:** Regrets of action fade; regrets of inaction compound. At age 80 the calculus is clear. Take the other path.

**Test:** Project to age 80. Which decision produces more regret: taking the risk and failing, or not taking the risk at all? If not-trying produces more regret, the path of no regrets points to action.

**Source:** Almanack

### Be a Free Agent, Not a Dependent
**Rule:** Have so much specific knowledge and clear value that you can leave anyone and the market absorbs you immediately.

**Test:** If this person left their current role tomorrow, how quickly would equivalent or better opportunities appear? If the honest answer is "months to years," they are a dependent. Free agents are absorbed immediately.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Pick Honesty as a Business Strategy
**Rule:** Every lie is debt repaid with interest. Be known as someone who tells hard truths diplomatically.

**Test:** Has this person told a hard truth to a client, partner, or investor in the last 3 months that risked the relationship but was necessary? If not, honesty as a strategy may be more aspiration than practice.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### Escape Competition Through Authenticity
**Rule:** You can't be copied exactly. The more authentically you operate, the more you escape competition entirely.

**Test:** Is the current work a combination of specific knowledge and personal approach that could not be replicated by someone who merely learned the skills? Authentic work is harder to copy than skillful work.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

## Layer 2 — Approach Taste
*Given the problem, which angle to attack from*

Naval's approach doctrine is organized around one meta-principle: compounding. Every tactic is evaluated by whether it compounds — skills, relationships, reputation, knowledge. Tactics that don't compound (credential-chasing, status-seeking, busyness) are explicitly deprioritized regardless of their short-term appeal.

### Read What You Love Until You Love to Read
**Rule:** Start with what genuinely interests you. Volume requires desire. 500 books in your domain of obsession beats 50 important books you didn't want to read.

**Test:** Is reading currently driven by genuine interest or by a sense of obligation ("I should read this")? Obligation-driven reading produces low retention. Interest-driven reading produces specific knowledge.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### Learn How to Learn
**Rule:** The meta-skill of learning — how your brain retains information, what study methods work — multiplies every other skill you develop.

**Test:** Can the person describe specifically how they learn best — what conditions, what methods, what timing? If the answer is vague, the meta-skill of learning has not been cultivated.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### The Clear Mind Prerequisite
**Rule:** Good judgment requires a clear mind. Meditation, reduced news consumption, and controlled inputs are prerequisites for clear thinking.

**Test:** How many hours per week does the person spend on deliberate inputs (news, social media, others' opinions) versus structured silence or reflection? High deliberate-input ratio degrades judgment quality.

**Source:** Almanack; Tim Ferriss #473

### Prioritize Health, Then Wealth, Then Love
**Rule:** Health is the foundation. The order matters: most people build in the wrong sequence.

**Test:** Is the person treating health as the foundation, or sacrificing it for wealth or status? Sustained health degradation is a tax on all downstream activities. Ask: what health metrics are being tracked?

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Meditation as Cognitive Maintenance
**Rule:** Meditation improves decision quality by reducing reactive thinking and increasing the space between stimulus and response.

**Test:** Does the person have a consistent meditation or reflection practice? Even 10 minutes of daily structured silence demonstrates the cognitive maintenance habit. No practice = reactive decision-making mode by default.

**Source:** Almanack; Tim Ferriss #473

### Judgment Over Effort
**Rule:** Work hard enough to stay in the game, but judgment — the quality of your decisions — matters more than quantity of effort.

**Test:** Over the last year, which produced more value: the person's total hours worked, or the quality of 3-5 key decisions? If good decisions are indistinguishable from bad ones in their reasoning quality, judgment isn't being cultivated.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### Use Mental Models Relentlessly
**Rule:** The breadth of your mental model library determines the quality of your cross-domain reasoning.

**Test:** When facing a new problem, can the person draw on models from physics, biology, economics, and psychology to frame it? Single-domain reasoning is the alternative. Cross-domain model use is observable in how they explain decisions.

**Source:** Almanack; Farnam Street; Naval podcast

### First Principles Over Analogy
**Rule:** Reasoning by analogy carries all the assumptions of the comparison case. First principles asks what must be true.

**Test:** For the last major decision, was the reasoning "this is like X" or "the fundamental requirements are Y"? Analogy-based reasoning is faster but imports the comparison's limitations.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Network Selectively and Deeply
**Rule:** Networking for truth — finding people who think clearly and will be honest — is invaluable. Networking for access is largely a waste.

**Test:** How many people in the person's network would tell them an uncomfortable truth without softening it? More than 5 truth-tellers = a high-quality network. A large network that only affirms is not valuable for decision quality.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### Build Products, Not Services
**Rule:** Services scale with your time. Products scale without it. Same effort, one product serves 100,000 people as well as one.

**Test:** What % of current income comes from products versus services? A service business growing revenue requires proportional time investment. A product business does not.

**Source:** Almanack

### Say No to Almost Everything
**Rule:** Every commitment crowds out the possibility of a better commitment. If not enthusiastically yes, it's a no.

**Test:** Apply the "enthusiastically yes" test to each current commitment. Any commitment that produces a "well, I should probably" response rather than a "yes!" is a candidate for elimination.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### The 40-Hour Week Is an Industrial Artifact
**Rule:** Knowledge work compounds unevenly. One week of deep insight can be worth 10 weeks of effort. Manage output, not time.

**Test:** Is work being measured by hours logged or by output produced? Hour-counting is the industrial-era frame. Output-counting is the knowledge-work frame. Misaligned measurement destroys value.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Micro-Decisions Compound
**Rule:** How you do anything is how you do everything. Small recurring decisions define the habits and reputation that compound into large outcomes.

**Test:** Identify the most frequently repeated small decision in the person's day. Does the pattern of that decision reflect the values and standards they want to compound into large outcomes?

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### Become the Best in the World at What You Do
**Rule:** Not top 1% — genuinely the world's best at a specific combination of skills. The internet connects you to everyone who needs exactly that.

**Test:** At the intersection of the person's specific knowledge stack, are they genuinely the world's best? If not, what additional specificity or depth would make them so? The goal is world's best at a narrow enough combination.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### The Honest Brag
**Rule:** Sharing knowledge and results publicly is market signal. Silence about valuable work is a market inefficiency.

**Test:** Is the person's best work visible to the people who would most benefit from it? Hiding valuable work under modesty is not humility — it's withholding signal from a market that needs it.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast; AngelList

### Play Iterated Games
**Rule:** In iterated games, cooperation pays. Structure professional life so all important relationships are iterated. Reputation is the shadow of iterated games.

**Test:** What % of the person's important professional relationships are with people they expect to interact with again in 5+ years? One-shot relationships are structurally less cooperative than iterated ones.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Embrace Intellectual Curiosity as an Asset
**Rule:** Curious people ask better questions, learn faster, and spot opportunities earlier. Cultivate it deliberately.

**Test:** In the last week, has the person followed a curious impulse into a domain they knew nothing about? Structured curiosity — deliberately following interesting threads — is an observable habit.

**Source:** Almanack; Tim Ferriss #97

### Block Time for Thinking
**Rule:** Protect large blocks of unscheduled time specifically for thinking — not reading, not outputting. Just thinking.

**Test:** How many hours per week are blocked exclusively for thinking — no phone, no reading, no output? Zero thinking time means all work is reactive. The thinking block is where compounding insight is generated.

**Source:** Almanack

### Write to Think
**Rule:** Writing forces clarity. The act of explaining an idea exposes logical gaps and hidden assumptions that feel resolved in your head.

**Test:** Has the person written out their core thesis or current major decision in longform in the last month? If the thinking only exists as internal monologue, the clarity test has not been run.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### The Single Virtue of Good Advisors
**Rule:** A good advisor tells you what you don't want to hear. The one who makes you feel good is providing comfort, not advice.

**Test:** In the last 3 advisor conversations, did any advisor say something the person didn't want to hear? If all advisor conversations were affirming, the advisors are providing comfort, not counsel.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Operate at Your Unique Energy Peak
**Rule:** Know your two best cognitive hours per day. Protect them for your most important work.

**Test:** Have the person's two peak cognitive hours been identified and are they scheduled exclusively for highest-leverage work? If peak hours are regularly consumed by email and meetings, the asset is being underutilized.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### The Two-Minute Rule for Small Decisions
**Rule:** Small decisions receive the same deliberation as large ones, which is cognitively wasteful. Decide fast on low stakes; spend time on high stakes.

**Test:** On the last 10 decisions made, was the time allocated proportional to the stakes involved? If small decisions consumed more than 5 minutes each, cognitive capital is being misspent.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Environment Design Beats Willpower
**Rule:** Design your environment so the right behaviors are default. Structure beats discipline.

**Test:** Are the person's most important habits the path of least resistance, or do they require active willpower to maintain? If willpower is the mechanism, the environment has not been designed.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### The Short-Cut Versus Long-Cut Frame
**Rule:** There is no shortcut to building genuine specific knowledge. The 'shortcuts' are the long cuts. The overnight success took 20 years.

**Test:** Does this person's specific knowledge look like it was built over years of obsessive engagement, or over months of deliberate acquisition? Quickly-acquired skills are not specific knowledge.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### Copy From Nature, Not From People
**Rule:** The best mental models come from physics, biology, mathematics. Nature's solutions are optimal; human solutions are suboptimal at best.

**Test:** When asked to explain a decision, does the person use analogies from nature (evolution, physics, game theory) or from other human examples? Nature-sourced models are more robust than human-example-sourced ones.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Choose Wealth-Building Over Signaling
**Rule:** Conspicuous consumption produces no wealth. The wealthiest people Naval knows often live simply.

**Test:** What fraction of after-tax income is going to signaling (visible consumption, status-maintaining expenses) versus asset accumulation? High signaling fraction is the anti-wealth pattern.

**Source:** Almanack

### Trust the Process, Not the Prediction
**Rule:** You cannot predict which specific bets pay off, but a good process — working with high-integrity people, building specific knowledge, deploying leverage — produces wealth over time.

**Test:** Is the person's wealth-building plan dependent on a specific bet paying off, or on running a good process repeatedly? Bet-dependent plans fail when bets fail. Process-dependent plans compound across many bets.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Be Impatient With Actions, Patient With Results
**Rule:** Act fast on decisions and experiments. Be patient with the compounding that follows. Most people have this exactly backward.

**Test:** How quickly does the person make and act on decisions once sufficient information exists? And how patient are they with the compounding that follows? Speed at acting + patience at waiting is the right combination.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

## Layer 3 — Stopping Taste
*When to abandon, when to push, when "done enough"*

Naval's stopping doctrine is surprisingly clear-cut: stop when you've lost genuine interest, when the game is zero-sum, when the environment cannot support your growth, or when the compounding has stopped. Hold when genuine compounding continues.

### Boredom Is a Signal, Not a Problem
**Rule:** Boredom means the compounding has stopped. It is information about fit, not a motivation problem to overcome with discipline.

**Test:** Is the person experiencing genuine intellectual boredom — not fatigue, not distraction, but actual lack of interest in the core work? If yes, the compounding has stopped. Discipline cannot substitute for absent compounding.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### If It's Not Hell Yes, It's No
**Rule:** For major commitments, if your response is not an unambiguous yes, it should be a no. 'Pretty good' crowds out great.

**Test:** Rate the current commitment on a 1-10 scale. If it is not a 9 or 10, ask what a 9-10 commitment would look like and whether this is crowding it out.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### Exit Toxic Environments Immediately
**Rule:** Environments that reward political behavior, punish honesty, or prevent the accumulation of specific knowledge cannot be fixed from inside. Exit.

**Test:** Does the environment reward political navigation more than actual output quality? If the answer is yes for more than 6 months, the environment is toxic and will not be fixed from inside.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Stop Optimizing for Appearance
**Rule:** When you catch yourself making decisions to look good rather than produce results, stop. The status game has infiltrated the wealth game.

**Test:** In the last 5 decisions, how many were optimized for how they would look to others versus what they would produce? More than 1 appearance-optimized decision in 5 is the status-game infection signal.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### The Sunk Cost Exit Rule
**Rule:** Time, money, reputation already spent cannot be recovered. The right question is always: given everything I know now, would I start this today?

**Test:** Remove all prior investment from consideration. Given current knowledge, would the current path be started from scratch today? If not, sunk cost is driving the continuation.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### The Addiction Test
**Rule:** For any habit, platform, or activity: can you put it down for a week without anxiety? If not, you have lost freedom of choice.

**Test:** Pick the platform or habit and stop it for 7 days. Does anxiety, compulsion, or discomfort arise? Any yes is the loss-of-freedom signal.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Hold When Compounding Is Real
**Rule:** Genuine compounding is often invisible short-term and dramatic long-term. Don't confuse slow visible progress with no progress.

**Test:** Is the current trajectory showing compounding characteristics — each year building on the last, even slowly? Or is it flat? Flat for 2+ years with no explanation is the real stopping signal.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### The Path Dependency Trap
**Rule:** Most people are on paths they chose years ago with less information. Periodically re-evaluate from first principles: is this the best allocation of your one life?

**Test:** Set a calendar reminder every 12 months to ask: knowing what I know now, is this the best use of the next 5 years? This question should be answered from scratch, not rationalized from the current trajectory.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Stop Status Games When You Catch Yourself Playing Them
**Rule:** Status games are recognizable: comparing to peers, competitive rather than curious, decisions based on rank. Notice and stop.

**Test:** In the last week, did any decision primarily reflect wanting to be above a peer or impress an audience? If yes, the status game has infiltrated the decision set. Name it and redirect.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### "I Don't Have Time" = "It's Not a Priority"
**Rule:** You have the same 168 hours as everyone else. 'No time' is a values statement in disguise. Acknowledge the true reason.

**Test:** For everything the person says they "don't have time for," translate it: "I have decided this is not a priority." Does the translation feel accurate? If it feels wrong, the priority hierarchy needs revision.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### The "Would I Hire Myself?" Test
**Rule:** For every role you fill: would you hire the person doing it? Apply this to yourself. Wrong role = move to one where you are the clear best person.

**Test:** If the person were interviewing themselves for their current role, would they be the top candidate? If not, they are in the wrong role. The right role is one where they are the obvious best hire.

**Source:** Almanack; AngelList

### Stop When Your Highest Leverage Isn't Here
**Rule:** Where is the highest-leverage use of your specific knowledge right now? If not where you are, that's your exit signal.

**Test:** Name the highest-leverage application of the person's specific knowledge globally. Is it the current role? If a clearly higher-leverage opportunity exists and isn't being pursued, staying is a leverage sacrifice.

**Source:** Almanack; tweetstorm

### Enough Is a Personal Definition
**Rule:** Define what enough looks like financially before you reach it. People who don't define enough never reach it.

**Test:** Can the person state a specific financial number that represents "enough" for them personally? If they cannot, the goal is undefined and they will continue optimizing past it indefinitely.

**Source:** Almanack; Joe Rogan #1309

### The One-Year Test for Regret
**Rule:** In one year, will you regret having left, or regret having stayed? The regret asymmetry is real.

**Test:** Simulate both branches 12 months forward. Which produces more regret? The branch with more projected regret is the one to avoid.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### Rebuild After Failure Immediately
**Rule:** Motion beats reflection in the aftermath of setbacks. The longer you wait after failure before the next bet, the harder it becomes.

**Test:** After the last significant failure, how long was the period of inaction before the next bet? Extended inaction post-failure is the failure compounding; immediate next action is the recovery pattern.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

### The Permissionless Exit
**Rule:** You don't need anyone's permission to stop. Exit social obligations and status-driven commitments without guilt.

**Test:** Are there current commitments maintained primarily because of what others would think if they ended? Name them. Exiting without permission from others is the permissionless exit.

**Source:** Almanack; @naval

### Choose Association Deliberately
**Rule:** You are the average of the people you spend the most time with. Choose intentionally; exit gracefully when the fit is wrong.

**Test:** List the 5 people the person spends the most time with. Is their average level of specific knowledge, wealth-building, and integrity at or above the person's current level? If consistently below, the association is pulling the average down.

**Source:** Almanack; Naval podcast

## 2026 Overlay

### Where the alpha is
- AI-enabled specific knowledge businesses: individuals with deep domain expertise + AI leverage = individual unicorns
- Permissionless media at scale: the individual brand is now more valuable than the institutional brand in many verticals
- Crypto/decentralized finance: permissionless wealth-building mechanisms that disintermediate traditional gatekeepers
- Physical world applications of software leverage: robotics, bio, energy — sectors where software leverage is newly applicable
- Individual-to-individual trust networks replacing institutional trust as AI makes institutional trust more fragile

### Auto-reject
- Credential-optimizing career paths where the credential is the moat
- Any business where the primary leverage is regulatory capture
- Zero-sum competitive markets where winning requires someone else to lose rather than new value created
- High-status, low-wealth positions (fancy job titles at large organizations without equity)
- Work that requires permanent busyness rather than deep thinking and judgment
